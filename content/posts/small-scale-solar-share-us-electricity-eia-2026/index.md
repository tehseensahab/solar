**Small-scale solar, most of it on rooftops, supplied an estimated 2.36% of U.S. electricity in January through July 2026**, up from 2.14% in the same months of 2025, according to our calculation from the U.S. Energy Information Administration's (EIA) Electric Power Monthly. Its generation rose 12.6% over that period, slower than the 22.3% growth in utility-scale solar photovoltaic (PV) generation.

All figures are for the United States, from the data tables EIA released September 24, 2026 (covering through July 2026). EIA labels 2025 and 2026 values preliminary.

## The Short Answer

| Measure | Jan.–July 2025 | Jan.–July 2026 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Small-scale solar PV generation (estimated) | 56,507 thousand MWh | 63,623 thousand MWh | +12.6% |
| Utility-scale solar PV generation | 175,343 thousand MWh | 214,427 thousand MWh | +22.3% |
| Utility-scale generation, all sources, plus small-scale solar | 2,635,189 thousand MWh | 2,693,522 thousand MWh | +2.2% |
| Small-scale solar share of that total (our calculation) | 2.14% | 2.36% | +0.22 percentage points |

One thousand MWh (megawatt-hours) equals one gigawatt-hour (GWh), so 63,623 thousand MWh is 63.6 million MWh, or 63.6 terawatt-hours (TWh). "Utility-scale" means power plants of 1 megawatt (MW) and larger that EIA surveys directly. The share is **small-scale solar divided by the sum of EIA's utility-scale total and its small-scale solar estimate.** Other small-scale resources, such as small wind, are not in the denominator.

## What "Small-Scale" Means, and How EIA Gets the Numbers

Small-scale generation does not come from meter readings. EIA's technical notes say: "Monthly generation from small scale PV solar resources is an estimation of the generation produced from PV solar resources and not the results of a data collection effort for generation directly," with an exception for third-party-owned (TPO) installations, where direct data are collected but are "not comprehensive."

Instead, EIA estimates generation from solar capacity that utilities report for net-metered customers, by state and customer class: residential, commercial and industrial. So this series is a modeled estimate. Differences between a state's estimate and its true output could exist, and the tables we used do not show an error range.

For January–July 2026, EIA's state table splits the 63,623 thousand MWh by customer sector:

| Sector | Estimated small-scale generation, Jan.–July 2026 | Share of small-scale total (our calculation) |
|---|---|---|
| Residential | 42,910 thousand MWh | 67.4% |
| Commercial | 17,365 thousand MWh | 27.3% |
| Industrial | 3,348 thousand MWh | 5.3% |

Residential small-scale generation grew 11.7% from 38,428 thousand MWh in January–July 2025.

## The Long View: Five-Fold Growth Since 2016

| Year | Small-scale solar PV generation (estimated) | Share of utility-scale plus small-scale generation (our calculation) |
|---|---|---|
| 2016 | 18,812 thousand MWh | 0.46% |
| 2020 | 41,522 thousand MWh | 1.02% |
| 2024 | 83,918 thousand MWh | 1.91% |
| 2025 (preliminary) | 93,148 thousand MWh | 2.06% |

From 2016 to 2025, small-scale solar generation grew about 4.95 times (93,148 divided by 18,812). From 2024 to 2025 it grew 11.0%. 2016 through 2024 values are final per EIA, and 2025 is preliminary.

## The Seasonal Pattern in 2026

Solar output rises into the summer, but demand rises too, so the share of electricity moves differently than the output:

| Month, 2026 | Small-scale solar (thousand MWh) | Share of total (our calculation) |
|---|---|---|
| January | 6,051 | 1.49% |
| February | 6,433 | 1.84% |
| March | 8,953 | 2.50% |
| April | 9,808 | 2.88% |
| May | 10,728 | 2.94% |
| June | 10,724 | 2.61% |
| July | 10,925 | 2.35% |

July 2026 had the most small-scale solar generation of any month in the data we reviewed (January 2024 through July 2026), at 10,925 thousand MWh. May 2026 had the highest share, 2.94%. The share fell in June and July because total generation climbed from 354,691 thousand MWh (utility-scale, May) to 453,744 thousand MWh (July) while solar output was roughly flat. This is our arithmetic, and the demand explanation is our interpretation.

## Which States Lead

EIA's state table shows small-scale generation for January–July 2026 and 2025. The 12 largest states, ranked by 2026 estimated small-scale generation:

| Rank | State | Jan.–July 2026 (thousand MWh) | Change from Jan.–July 2025 | Share of U.S. small-scale total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | California | 23,021 | +11.0% | 36.2% |
| 2 | Arizona | 3,745 | +7.2% | 5.9% |
| 3 | New York | 3,676 | +15.3% | 5.8% |
| 4 | Florida | 3,566 | +12.6% | 5.6% |
| 5 | Texas | 3,492 | +6.9% | 5.5% |
| 6 | Massachusetts | 2,890 | +11.1% | 4.5% |
| 7 | New Jersey | 2,672 | +15.7% | 4.2% |
| 8 | Illinois | 1,826 | +33.1% | 2.9% |
| 9 | Colorado | 1,561 | +16.7% | 2.5% |
| 10 | Nevada | 1,501 | +9.9% | 2.4% |
| 11 | Connecticut | 1,279 | +19.1% | 2.0% |
| 12 | Maryland | 1,240 | +20.0% | 1.9% |

Three cautions on reading this table:

- **It ranks volume, not penetration.** A large state ranks high regardless of how much of its power is solar. For example, in New Jersey and Massachusetts, estimated small-scale solar was about 67% and 64% of each state's total solar generation, while in Texas it was about 7% because Texas has much more utility-scale solar.
- **Growth rates are from a small base in some states.** Illinois's +33.1% reflects 1,826 versus 1,372 thousand MWh. EIA marks very large or noisy changes "NM" (not meaningful), so the table can change when estimates are revised.
- **State figures include commercial and industrial systems,** not only homes. Residential-only estimates are available in EIA's table by sector, for example California 15,595 and New York 1,601 thousand MWh in January–July 2026.

## Why It Matters, and What the Data Cannot Tell You

Small-scale solar is still a small slice of national generation, and utility-scale solar grew faster in this period. The numbers show how much electricity customer-sited solar is estimated to supply, not whether that solar is saving its owners money. Growth in 2026 came in the year the 30% federal homeowner credit ended (see [what federal credit remains](/posts/home-solar-federal-tax-credit-after-2025-leases-ppas/)). The EIA tables do not tell us why installations or generation changed, so we draw no causal conclusion.

## Limitations

- Small-scale generation is an EIA estimate based on reported capacity, not measured output. 2025 and 2026 values are preliminary and may be revised.
- The share we report uses EIA's utility-scale total plus its small-scale solar estimate. EIA does not publish a combined total of all generation including small-scale in the table we used, so another definition would give a slightly different percentage.
- State rows combine residential, commercial and industrial systems. Some state-level cells are marked NM (not meaningful) by EIA.
- We downloaded the tables on October 6, 2026. The next Electric Power Monthly release will update them.
