**A home's most-used TV costs about $38 a year to run, or about $3.18 a month.** That uses the Energy Information Administration's (EIA) 2020 survey average of 210 kWh a year for the most-used TV and EIA's U.S. average residential price of 18.16 cents per kWh for August 2026. Size and screen type matter: two current TVs we checked are rated at 143 kWh a year (43-inch) and 338 kWh a year (65-inch), which is about $26 and $61 a year, or roughly 1.4 and 3.4 cents per hour of viewing.

A TV is a small load next to heating, cooling or a water heater, but most homes have more than one, and cable boxes and game consoles add to the total. This guide shows the math and what changes it. For the units, see [kW vs kWh](/posts/kw-vs-kwh/), and for where a TV fits in a home's total use, see [how much electricity a house uses](/posts/how-much-electricity-does-a-house-use/).

## The Formula

**Cost = kWh used × your price per kWh**

For a TV: **kWh = watts ÷ 1,000 × hours on**. Your price per kWh is on your bill. If your bill splits supply and delivery, add the per-kWh parts together and leave out the fixed monthly customer charge.

## How Much Does It Cost to Run a TV?

EIA's 2020 Residential Energy Consumption Survey (RECS) reports average yearly electricity use per household that has each TV. At EIA's August 2026 U.S. average residential price of 18.16 cents per kWh (released Oct. 23, 2026; one month's price, not a yearly average):

| TV (EIA 2020 averages) | kWh per year | Cost per year | Cost per month |
|---|---|---|---|
| Most-used TV | 210 | $38.14 | $3.18 |
| Second TV | 106 | $19.25 | $1.60 |
| Third TV | 82 | $14.89 | $1.24 |
| All TVs and related equipment | 632 | $114.77 | $9.56 |

How we calculated it: kWh × $0.1816. Monthly figures are the yearly cost ÷ 12. EIA's "all TVs and related" category is broader than the TV sets alone and appears to include connected devices such as boxes and consoles. Averages are for homes that use each item.

## How Much Does It Cost to Run a TV per Hour?

The quickest way is the yellow EnergyGuide label. TV labels assume "5 hours in on mode and 19 hours in standby (sleep) mode per day," so the yearly kWh on the label covers 1,825 hours of viewing a year (5 × 365). Divide the label's kWh by 1,825 to get kWh per viewing hour.

| Example TV (EnergyGuide label) | Label kWh per year | kWh per viewing hour | Cost per hour at 18.16¢ | Cost per year at 18.16¢ | Label cost (16¢) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| TCL 43S350G, 40–44 inch class | 143 | 0.078 | 1.4¢ | $25.97 | $23 |
| Sony K-65XR80M2, 65–69 inch class | 338 | 0.185 | 3.4¢ | $61.38 | $55* |

*The Sony label's $55 is slightly above 338 kWh × 16¢ ($54.08), likely from rounding on the label. How we calculated it: label kWh ÷ 1,825 hours = kWh per viewing hour (this folds the small standby use into viewing hours). Cost per year = label kWh × $0.1816. These are single models, not averages; your TV may use more or less.

If you know your TV's power draw in watts, the per-hour cost is quick to work out:

| TV power while on | Cost per hour at 18.16¢ | Cost per year at 5 hours a day |
|---|---|---|
| 50 watts | 0.9¢ | $16.57 |
| 100 watts | 1.8¢ | $33.14 |
| 150 watts | 2.7¢ | $49.71 |
| 200 watts | 3.6¢ | $66.28 |

How we calculated it: watts ÷ 1,000 × $0.1816 per hour; yearly = watts ÷ 1,000 × 5 × 365 × $0.1816. Standby use is not included in this table.

## How to Read a TV's EnergyGuide Label

The Federal Trade Commission's labeling rule, 16 CFR 305.25, sets two assumptions for the "Estimated Yearly Energy Cost" on a TV label:

- **Usage:** 5 hours on and 19 hours in standby a day.
- **Price:** "an electricity cost rate of 16 cents per kWh." The FTC updated the TV label to this figure in a rule published Feb. 2, 2024, "based on the most recent national electricity cost information published by DOE." Manufacturers had to use the new figures on labels for TVs made from May 2, 2024.

Because the U.S. average was 18.16 cents in August 2026, the label's dollar figure is now about 12% below what the same kWh would cost at that price (our math: 18.16 ÷ 16 = 1.135). The kWh figure is the better number to use: multiply it by your own rate.

The label also shows a cost range for TVs of the same size class. Selected ranges from the FTC's 2024 table, at 16 cents per kWh:

| Screen size | Yearly cost range on label | About this many kWh per year (our math) |
|---|---|---|
| 40–44 inches | $13 – $51 | 81 – 319 |
| 50–54 inches | $22 – $51 | 138 – 319 |
| 55–59 inches | $24 – $69 | 150 – 431 |
| 65–69 inches | $27 – $110 | 169 – 688 |
| 69.5 inches or larger | $32 – $155 | 200 – 969 |

How we calculated it: dollars ÷ $0.16. The spread within one size class is wide, so two TVs of the same size can differ several times over in running cost.

## How Many Hours Do People Watch TV?

EIA's 2020 survey asked how long the most-used TV is on. On weekdays, the largest group of homes (41.66 million) reported 4 to 6 hours, and 19.17 million reported more than 10 hours. On weekends, 32.17 million homes reported 7 to 10 hours and 23.28 million more than 10 hours.

If your TV is on 10 hours a day instead of 5, double the label's yearly kWh. For the Sony 65-inch example, that is about 676 kWh, or about $123 a year at 18.16 cents (our math).

## Does a TV Use Electricity When It's Off?

A little. ENERGY STAR says "ENERGY STAR certified televisions must consume 0.5 watts or less in Sleep Mode." At 0.5 watts for 19 hours a day, that is about 3.5 kWh a year, or about 63 cents at 18.16 cents per kWh (our math). Devices around the TV, such as cable or satellite boxes, game consoles and sound bars, also draw standby power. A switched power strip turns them all off together.

## How to Cut the Cost of Running a TV

1. **Check the label kWh when you buy.** In the same size class, the FTC's ranges show a several-fold difference between the most and least efficient models.
2. **Look for ENERGY STAR.** ENERGY STAR says certified TVs are "on average, about 34% more energy efficient than conventional models."
3. **Stream on the TV itself.** ENERGY STAR says "Smart TVs are the most efficient way to stream."
4. **Try the energy-saving picture mode.** Many TVs have one. A plug-in power meter shows whether it lowers the watts your TV draws.
5. **Turn it off when no one is watching.** Hours matter more than anything else in the formula.

**With solar:** much TV viewing happens in the evening, after panels stop producing, so a TV then runs on grid power or credits from earlier in the day. Under full net metering, daytime surplus covers it kWh for kWh. Under export rates that pay less than retail, evening use like this is what a [home battery](/posts/how-to-size-a-home-battery/) can shift onto your own solar.

## Limitations

- EIA's kWh figures are from the 2020 survey, the latest with end-use detail. Today's TVs may use more or less than the 2020 averages.
- The two EnergyGuide labels are single models from retailer-hosted copies. We did not test any TV.
- 18.16 cents is one month's U.S. average. Your price may be much higher or lower; use your own bill.
