Plug-in solar, sometimes called balcony solar, is a small solar system you connect to your home through an ordinary electrical outlet instead of having it wired in by an electrician. Whether you may do that, and how big the system can be, depends on your state. We read the full statute text in three states, **Utah, Maine and Vermont**, and compared them. The short version: all three exempt qualifying systems from utility interconnection agreements and approval, but they differ on size, certification, installer requirements and who can use them.

This article covers state law in those three states only. It does not cover other states' laws, local building rules or homeowners' association rules, and it is not an installation guide.

## Utah: The Earliest of the Three, With the Fewest Conditions

Utah's H.B. 340 defines a "portable solar generation device" as a moveable photovoltaic generation device that has a maximum power output of not more than 1,200 watts, is designed to connect through a standard 120-volt alternating current outlet, is intended mainly to offset part of the customer's consumption, meets the most recent National Electrical Code, and is certified by Underwriters Laboratories or an equivalent nationally recognized testing laboratory.

A qualifying device is exempt from Utah's interconnection requirements, the requirement to sign an interconnection agreement, and the net metering program. It must include a feature that prevents it from energizing the building's wiring during a power outage. The utility, an "electrical corporation" in the statute, may not require approval, fees or additional controls, and is not liable for damage or injury caused by the device. The bill took effect May 7, 2025.

Note that Utah's text names UL certification generally; it does not name UL 3700, the plug-in-specific standard the other two states reference.

## Maine: Two Tiers

Maine's statute defines an "eligible system" as a plug-in photovoltaic or plug-in battery system with an export capacity of 1,200 watts or less that is certified to UL 3700 (or comparable standards) or is configured under the state-adopted National Electrical Code. Plug-in photovoltaic systems must use inverters configured to shut off after 0.2 seconds if power is disrupted.

- **Up to 420 watts** of combined inverter output (measured in alternating current) per service address: you may install and operate one or more eligible systems.
- **Above 420 and up to 1,200 watts:** each system must be installed by an electrician licensed in Maine and use a dedicated circuit with a single outlet, and you must notify your utility within 30 days of installation, with your service address, inverter capacity and a statement of compliance.

Eligible systems may not be used for net energy billing. The utility may not require approval, an interconnection application, agreement or study, fees, or extra equipment, and is not liable for damage or injury. If you install on a structure you do not own, you must make sure the installation does not compromise its integrity or violate building, fire or zoning codes, and restore the structure when the system is removed.

**Effective date.** The statute page we read does not give a date. Trade publication pv magazine USA reported on April 16, 2026 that Governor Janet Mills signed LD 1730 on April 6, 2026 and that, because non-emergency Maine laws take effect 90 days after the Legislature adjourns, the law's effective date falls "on or after July 15th." We did not confirm the exact date from a state source. Because the statute now appears in the Maine Revised Statutes, check the legislature's site for the current status.

## Vermont: The Most Detailed Consumer Rules

Vermont's Act 149 defines a plug-in photovoltaic device as one designed to connect via a cord plugged into a receptacle, with a feature that prevents energizing the building during an outage, that complies with UL 3700 or an equivalent certification by an equivalent nationally recognized testing laboratory and is installed and operated in compliance with IEEE 1547-2018, and that is connected to a building that is on the grid.

What the act says you may do and must do:

- Install **one or more devices per electric meter** with a combined inverter capacity of no more than 1,200 watts.
- Connect only to systems that use **smart meters** (meters that record usage digitally). The act says plug-in devices "shall only be connected to systems using smart meters."
- Attach the device "temporarily but securely" to the ground or a structure.
- If you already have a **net metering** system, you may not also install a plug-in device. Plug-in devices are not eligible for net metering, and exported power is not compensated.

What utilities may not do: require approval, charge any fee related to installation, or require additional controls beyond what is built into the device. The act does allow a utility to recover costs associated with overloading of its service caused by a plug-in device.

**Tenants and landlords.** A tenant must give the landlord at least 10 days' notice. The landlord must respond within 10 days with any reasonable restrictions, which may include requiring the tenant to pay for electrical work and hire a licensed electrician. If the landlord does not respond within 10 days, the tenant may proceed. A tenant may not do or hire electrical work without the landlord's permission, and a landlord cannot be compelled to perform or pay for electrical work.

**Towns and deed restrictions.** The act bars municipal zoning bylaws from regulating the installation, operation and maintenance of plug-in devices, and adds plug-in devices to the state law that prevents deed restrictions and covenants from blocking renewable energy devices. The act's amended text also says that law does not apply to patio railings in condominiums, cooperatives or apartments, "except for a plug-in photovoltaic device."

## Other States: Reported, Not Verified Here

Other states are moving. The Colorado General Assembly's bill page for **HB26-1007** shows the governor signed it on May 7, 2026 (Session Law Chapter 127, effective August 12, 2026) and says it bars retail electric and wholesale energy providers from requiring approval before a customer installs or uses a portable-scale solar generation device. We did not read the bill text, so we are not stating its wattage cap or certification terms. A New York State Senate press release says plug-in solar bills have become law in Utah, Maine, Virginia and Colorado, and that New York's SUNNY Act had passed the Legislature and was awaiting the governor's signature. We did not confirm a signing. If your state is not listed here, ask your utility and check your legislature's website; trackers online disagree on the state count.

## What Can It Actually Do for Your Bill?

A plug-in system offsets electricity you would otherwise buy. In Maine and Vermont, nothing you send back to the grid is credited, so the value depends on how much of the output you use at the moment it is produced.

**Illustrative arithmetic, not a measured result or a promise.** Assume a system produces the equivalent of 4 hours of full output per day, with 20% losses (a factor of 0.8), and that you use all of the power yourself. Assume the national average residential price the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) forecast for 2026, 18.2 cents per kilowatt-hour (see our [EIA October outlook article](/posts/eia-october-2026-electricity-price-forecast/)). Your own price, sun exposure, shading, orientation and usage will differ.

| System size | Daily output (kWh) | Value per day | Value per year |
|---|---|---|---|
| 420 W | 0.42 kW × 4 h × 0.8 = 1.34 kWh | about $0.24 | about $89 |
| 1,200 W | 1.2 kW × 4 h × 0.8 = 3.84 kWh | about $0.70 | about $255 |

These figures are before the cost of the equipment and any installation. Compare them with the product price and, if a licensed electrician is required (Maine above 420 watts), their fee. Our guides to [net metering](/posts/net-metering-state-guide/), [time-of-use rates](/posts/time-of-use-rates-and-solar/) and [solar payback without the federal tax credit](/posts/solar-payback-period-without-federal-tax-credit/) explain how bill credits and rate design change the math for larger rooftop systems.

## The Practical Hurdle: Certified Products

Vermont requires UL 3700 or an equivalent. Maine accepts UL 3700, a comparable standard, or an installation configured under the National Electrical Code. UL 3700 is a plug-in-specific safety standard; the trade outlet Electrek reported that it was published in December 2025 and, on July 20, 2026, that Hoymiles's HiFlow Pro was "the first US microinverter certified to meet UL 3700." That report describes one component, not a complete kit, and we could not verify a count of certified products independently. Before you buy, ask the seller for the certification listing, confirm the product meets your state's standard, and confirm it is allowed by your landlord, building and local code.

## Before You Buy: A Checklist

1. **Confirm your state law** (above) and read the statute for the size cap.
2. **Check certification** against what your state names (UL 3700 in Vermont and Maine).
3. **Check your meter.** Vermont limits plug-in devices to smart-meter systems. Ask your utility if you are unsure.
4. **Renters:** follow your state's notice process and get landlord agreement in writing.
5. **Do not combine** a plug-in device with other solar in states that bar it (Vermont bars it for customers with net metering).
6. **Run the numbers** with your own rate and sun hours; consider our [solar quote comparison guide](/posts/how-to-compare-solar-quotes/) if you are also pricing a conventional system.
