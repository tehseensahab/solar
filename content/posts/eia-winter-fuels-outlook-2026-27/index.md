The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) forecasts that households that heat mainly with electricity will spend an average of $1,196 on electricity from November 2026 through March 2027. That is 4% more than last winter. EIA's Winter Fuels Outlook 2026–27 was published on October 6, 2026 as part of its October Short-Term Energy Outlook, with a summary article on October 7.

The same outlook shows gas, propane and oil homes moving in different directions. Here is what the forecast says, what it assumes, and where EIA's own page does not agree with itself.

## The Forecast by Heating Fuel

EIA sorts households by the fuel they mainly use to heat their home. Spending is the average total bill for that fuel from November through March, not only the part used for heating.

| Main heating fuel | Homes using it, October 2026 (thousands) | Share of U.S. households | Average spending, Nov 2026–Mar 2027 | Change from last winter |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Natural gas | 60,962 | 45.4% | $640 | −9% |
| Electricity | 57,880 | 43.1% | $1,196 | +4% |
| Propane | 6,361 | 4.7% | $1,246 | −3% |
| Heating oil | 4,134 | 3.1% | $2,115 | +21% |

Homes using wood, other fuels or no heating (a combined 5,014 thousand) are not forecast. The share column is our calculation: each fuel's household count divided by the sum of all six categories in EIA's household table, 134,351 thousand. EIA's own text says natural gas heats 46% of homes (using Census data for 2024) and that more than 40% of homes heat with electricity, which is consistent with our figures.

Dollar amounts compare unlike things. A natural gas home and a heating oil home live in different parts of the country, with different climates and home sizes. Do not read the table as "oil costs more than gas for the same house."

## Electric-Heat Homes: What Drives the 4%

For homes heating with electricity, EIA's table gives an average of 6,562 kilowatt-hours (kWh) over the five months at an average price of 18.23 cents per kWh, which multiplies to $1,196. That is an average of about 1,312 kWh a month (6,562 ÷ 5), and about $239 a month ($1,196 ÷ 5).

| Month | Average spending, electric-heat homes |
|---|---|
| November 2026 | $213 |
| December 2026 | $253 |
| January 2027 | $270 |
| February 2027 | $230 |
| March 2027 | $230 |
| **Five-month total** | **$1,196** |

EIA's prose says higher spending is "primarily driven by higher retail electricity prices," with a milder-winter assumption lowering use slightly. This ties to our [coverage of EIA's October price outlook](/posts/eia-october-2026-electricity-price-forecast/), which forecast the 2026 average U.S. residential price (all homes, all months) at 18.2 cents per kWh. The 18.23-cent winter figure above is a different measure, covering November through March for electric-heat homes, so the two should not be compared directly.

## Heating Oil: The Largest Increase

EIA expects heating oil households, about 3% of U.S. homes and mostly in the Northeast, to spend an average of $2,115, up 21%. The table shows the price rising 34% to $5.26 per gallon, while use falls 9% to 402 gallons. EIA says warmer Northeast temperatures offset part of the price increase.

EIA's explanation is global. Reduced global refining activity has cut distillate production and raised international prices. U.S. distillate exports rose 20% in the first seven months of 2026 compared with the same period of 2025, and EIA expects U.S. distillate inventories to be about 11% below the five-year average in the fourth quarter of 2026.

## Natural Gas and Propane

EIA forecasts lower spending for both. Natural gas homes would pay an average of $640, 9% less, and propane homes $1,246, 3% less. EIA's tables show a U.S. average propane price of $2.52 per gallon, down 2%.

## The Weather Assumption

EIA's forecast uses heating degree days (HDDs). An HDD measures how far a day's average temperature falls below 65°F: a day averaging 50°F counts as 15 HDDs. EIA population-weights them. Its base case for November through March 2026–27:

| Region | HDDs, Nov–Mar | Change from last winter |
|---|---|---|
| Northeast | 4,331 | −12% |
| Midwest | 4,802 | −2% |
| South | 2,021 | +1% |
| West | 2,781 | +25% |
| U.S. | 3,155 | 0% |

The national total is flat, but the regions differ sharply. The Northeast is forecast to be milder than a relatively cold last winter, and the West colder than an unusually warm one, according to EIA. EIA also runs side cases in which every region is 10% colder or 10% warmer. A colder winter would be expected to raise these figures and a warmer one to lower them. We did not report the side-case numbers.

## Where EIA's Own Page Does Not Agree With Itself

When we compared the narrative text on EIA's Winter Fuels Outlook page with its tables, we found conflicts. We chose the figures that match the October 7 summary article and the tables' internal arithmetic (for example, 18.23 cents × 6,562 kWh equals $1,196), and we left out the rest.

| Item | Tables and October 7 summary | Narrative text on the outlook page |
|---|---|---|
| Natural gas spending, U.S. | −9% ($640) | "Close to last winter's levels" |
| Electricity price, electric-heat homes | +3% (18.23¢ per kWh) | "5% increase" |
| Propane price | $2.52 per gallon, −2% | $2.46 per gallon, −7% |

We have not asked EIA which is correct. We also left out EIA's regional dollar figures for the same reason, because the narrative and tables disagree there too. EIA's total of electricity spending (+4%) agrees across the summary, the table and the text.

## What This Means for Solar Owners

- **A solar system lowers the kWh you buy, not the whole bill.** Fixed monthly charges stay on your bill. See [fixed charges and solar savings](/posts/fixed-charges-and-solar-savings/).
- **Solar output generally falls in winter because days are shorter**, though the effect varies by region. This is general background: we have not quantified it here, and EIA's outlook does not cover solar.
- **How exports are credited matters more when winter bills are high.** Credit rules differ by utility. See [export credit rates by utility](/posts/solar-export-credit-rates-by-utility-2026/).
- **If you heat with electricity, check your plan.** Ask your utility whether a different rate plan fits your use. Your own bill will not match a national average. Our guide on [how to read your electric bill](/posts/how-to-read-your-electric-bill/) explains the lines to check, and our [rates by state](/rates-by-state/) pages cover pending changes.

## Limitations

These are EIA forecasts completed October 1, 2026 and published October 6, 2026. EIA says it will update the winter figures with each monthly outlook through March; the next Short-Term Energy Outlook is scheduled for November 10, 2026. All forecasts depend on weather and on fuel prices that can move. Averages hide wide variation by home size, efficiency and location. We reported national averages only. We did not verify the household counts independently of EIA's table.
