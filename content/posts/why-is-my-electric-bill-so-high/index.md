A high electric bill often comes from **using more kilowatt-hours (kWh)** rather than from a higher rate. Common causes are hot or cold weather, a billing period with more days, an estimated meter reading that was later corrected, or one appliance running more than you think. Rates matter too: EIA put the U.S. average residential price at 18.16¢ per kWh in August 2026, up 3.4% from a year earlier.

This guide walks through eight causes in the order worth checking, using the numbers already printed on your bill. If you need help with the lines on the bill itself, start with our guide to [reading your electric bill](/posts/how-to-read-your-electric-bill/).

## Step 1: Separate usage from price

Before looking for causes, find two numbers on this bill and on the bill from the same month last year: **total kWh** and **total dollars**. Then divide dollars by kWh to get your **all-in cost per kWh**.

- **kWh up, cost per kWh about the same:** you used more power. Go to causes 1 to 5.
- **kWh about the same, cost per kWh up:** something in the price changed. Go to causes 6 to 8.
- **Both up:** both are in play, which is common in winter and summer.

Compare with the same month last year, not last month. Use swings with the seasons, so a January bill compared with an October bill tells you little.

## Usage causes

### 1. Weather: heating and cooling

Heating and cooling are among the largest home uses of electricity, and they change most with the weather. EIA says the three largest uses of electricity in U.S. homes in 2020 were **air conditioning (19%)**, **space heating (12%)** and **water heating (12%)**, with lighting and refrigerators next. EIA also notes that homes in the South are more likely to have electric heat and use more air conditioning, and that these shares change from year to year with the weather.

So a hot summer or a cold snap can raise a bill with no change in your habits. Homes heated with electricity feel winter most; our [winter heating cost outlook](/posts/winter-heating-costs-2026-27/) covers what EIA expects this season.

### 2. A longer billing period

Bills don't always cover the same number of days. Dominion Energy, for example, tells customers that the number of days "may change from month to month because of weekends and holidays." The effect is bigger than it sounds:

| Billing period | Days | Change vs. 28 days |
|---|---|---|
| Short cycle | 28 | — |
| Typical | 30 | +7% |
| Long cycle | 35 | +25% |

To compare fairly, divide kWh by the number of days to get **kWh per day**. A home using EIA's national average of about 899 kWh a month (2022) uses about 30 kWh a day.

### 3. An estimated meter reading

Sometimes a utility can't read the meter. Dominion says this can happen "because of weather, access problems, or technical issues," and that when an actual reading follows, "your bill may be updated." If an estimate was too low, the next actual reading picks up the difference, and that bill looks unusually high. Many bills mark estimated reads, sometimes with an "E"; check your utility's bill guide. If several estimates in a row were followed by a big catch-up bill, ask the utility to review the account and offer a payment plan.

### 4. One appliance doing more than you think

The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) gives a simple formula:

**(Wattage × hours used per day) ÷ 1,000 = kWh per day**

Multiply by the days you use it, then by your price per kWh. Two examples at the August 2026 U.S. average of 18.16¢ (your rate will differ):

| Appliance | Assumptions | kWh | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Space heater | 1,500 W, 6 hours a day, 30 days | 270 kWh a month | About $49 a month |
| Electric kettle | 1,500 W, 1 hour a day, 365 days (DOE's example) | 547.5 kWh a year | About $99 a year |

DOE's own kettle example used 11¢ per kWh and came to $60.23 a year; at 18.16¢ the same use costs about $99.

For plug-in devices, DOE suggests an electricity usage monitor, which works on standard 120-volt outlets but not on 240-volt appliances. For a refrigerator, which cycles on and off, DOE says to divide the time it's plugged in by three to estimate hours at full wattage. DOE also notes that many devices draw standby power when switched "off," which you can avoid by unplugging them or switching off a power strip.

### 5. Something new in the house

A new electric vehicle, a hot tub, a second refrigerator, a home office or a new household member can each add use that stays on every bill. If your kWh per day rose and stayed up, look for a change that started that month.

## Price causes

### 6. A rate increase or a new rider

Utilities change base rates through rate cases, and many bills also carry riders for fuel, storms or programs that change on their own schedule. EIA's August 2026 figure, 18.16¢ per kWh, was up 3.4% from August 2025 nationally. If your cost per kWh rose much more than that, look on the bill for a new or larger rider, or check your state's rate picture on our [electric rates by state](/rates-by-state/) page. Our explainer on [how electric rate cases work](/posts/how-electric-rate-cases-work/) shows how increases reach bills.

### 7. Supply price or a contract that ended

In states with retail choice, the supply (generation) part of your bill can come from a supplier. If a fixed-price contract ended and rolled into a variable rate, the supply line can jump with no change in use. Compare that line with your utility's price to compare, as we explain in [comparing supplier offers](/posts/how-to-compare-electricity-supplier-offers/).

### 8. Time-of-use or demand charges

If you are on a time-of-use plan, the same kWh cost more in peak hours, so shifting laundry or EV charging into the peak raises the bill. Some plans also charge for your highest demand in a short interval. See [time-of-use rates](/posts/time-of-use-rates-and-solar/) and [residential demand charges](/posts/residential-demand-charges-explained/) to check whether either applies to you.

## What to do next

1. **Check the bill for errors:** the meter number, the read type (actual or estimated), the number of days and the rate plan name.
2. **Call the utility if something doesn't add up.** Ask whether the reading was estimated and whether a usage history or interval data is available online.
3. **Spread the cost if you need to.** [Budget billing](/posts/budget-billing-explained/) evens out monthly payments, though it doesn't lower your annual cost. If you're behind, see our guide to [energy bill assistance](/posts/liheap-energy-assistance-2026-27/).
4. **Then consider bigger fixes.** Weatherizing, a more efficient heating system or solar can cut use or cost for years. Solar reduces the energy part of the bill but not fixed customer charges, as we explain in [fixed charges and solar savings](/posts/fixed-charges-and-solar-savings/).
