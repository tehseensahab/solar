For cold climates, look at the **lowest rated operating temperature** and **HSPF2**. On published figures, **Carrier Infinity** and **Rheem Endeavor Classic Plus** list the coldest limit (-23°F), and **Mitsubishi Hyper Heat** lists the highest HSPF2 (up to 13.5). Daikin Aurora and Trane's cold-climate model list -13°F. We did not test any heat pump. Figures come from EnergySage's table (updated November 4, 2025), the NEEP specification, ENERGY STAR and DOE, read October 24, 2026. See [how we pick products](/how-we-pick/).

## What "cold climate" means

- **NEEP specification (v3.1, effective August 1, 2021):** COP greater than 1.75 at 5°F at maximum capacity; HSPF above 10 for non-ducted or above 9 for ducted systems; SEER above 15. Manufacturers report capacity and COP at 47°F, 17°F, 5°F and the lowest cataloged temperature. We read this version; check [NEEP](https://neep.org/) for newer ones.
- **ENERGY STAR:** says cold-climate units go through third-party verified testing down to 5°F, can keep running below that, and that pairing them with backup heat can be most efficient at lower temperatures.
- **DOE:** says a properly installed heat pump can deliver up to three times more heat energy than the electricity it uses, recommends the highest HSPF feasible in colder climates and a demand-defrost control, and advises shielding the outdoor unit from high winds.

## What does it cost, and what might it save?

EnergySage reported that whole-home heat pump installations averaged about $19,000 in 2025, or roughly $16,500 after state and local tax credits and rebates (cold-climate guide, Nov 4, 2025). Its separate guide, updated June 29, 2026, puts the average new heat pump at $15,393 before incentives, with most installs between $14,353 and $25,957, based on marketplace quotes. These figures mix system types and scopes, so they do not match; use them as a range and get quotes.

Savings: DOE cites a NEEP study of cold-climate units in the Northeast and Mid-Atlantic that found average annual savings of about 3,000 kWh ($459) versus electric resistance heating and about 6,200 kWh ($948) versus oil systems. Those dollar figures reflect that study's prices, not today's. Your result depends on your fuel prices; see [Winter Heating Costs 2026–27](/posts/winter-heating-costs-2026-27/) and [Rates by State](/rates-by-state/). We do not guarantee savings.

## Sizing in cold climates

DOE's Building America sizing guideline says heat pumps should be sized to a Manual J load, and in heating-dominated climates heating capacity may exceed the load by up to 25%, versus 15% in cooling-dominated ones. It also recommends a balance-point diagram to size supplemental and emergency heat. Ask your installer for a written Manual J load calculation; see [how to choose a heat pump](/posts/how-to-choose-a-heat-pump/).

## Who should skip it (or wait)

- Homes with leaky envelopes or undersized or leaky ducts; fix those first.
- Homes where the electricity-to-fuel price ratio makes heating more expensive; compare costs per unit of heat.
- Buyers who expect a federal credit (see below).
- Anyone whose installer will not provide a Manual J calculation.

## Incentives

The IRS says the [energy efficient home improvement credit](https://www.irs.gov/credits-deductions/energy-efficient-home-improvement-credit) applies to improvements made through December 31, 2025, and had a $2,000 annual heat pump limit. EnergySage's June 2026 guide says the federal credit is gone and state rebate programs still pay out in many areas. A June 2026 [state Home Energy Rebates factsheet](https://building-performance.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/06.2026-State-Home-Energy-Rebates-Factsheet.pdf) reports that twelve states and DC have launched rebate programs (the document also says thirteen in its introduction) and that the rebates target low- and moderate-income households. Check your state's energy office and utility.

## Buying checklist

1. Lowest outdoor temperature you must heat at, and your design load.
2. Ducted or ductless, and the condition of existing ducts.
3. HSPF2 and rated capacity at 5°F for the exact model number.
4. Backup heat plan.
5. Manual J in writing; panel capacity.
6. Warranty and installer reputation. See [how to compare quotes](/posts/how-to-compare-solar-quotes/) for a similar quote checklist.
7. State, utility and local rebates.
