Iowa did not have a base rate change on October 1, 2026. The state's two large utilities are in different positions: Alliant Energy is under a base rate freeze, and MidAmerican Energy is seeking an increase.

This article covers Alliant Energy (Interstate Power and Light) and MidAmerican Energy. It does not cover cooperatives or municipal utilities.

## Quick Summary

| Item | Utility | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Base rates | Alliant | Changed October 2024; frozen until at least late 2029 |
| Riders | Alliant | Changing in 2026; bills expected to rise |
| Proposed rate increase | MidAmerican | Docket RPU-2026-0001; comment meetings Sept. 8 to 10, 2026 |
| October 1, 2026 | Both | No base rate change found |

## Alliant: Frozen Base Rates, Moving Riders

Alliant's Iowa outlook page says new base rates took effect in October 2024, and that a moratorium "keeps base rates stable until at least late-2029."

It also says overall 2026 electric bills are expected to rise for residential customers, because of:

- **The Renewable Energy Rider ending,** with its costs moving into base rates.
- **The Tax Benefit Rider expiring** on October 1, 2025. That rider had been lowering bills.
- **A higher Regional Transmission Service charge.**
- Partly offset by an expected decrease in the Energy Adjustment Clause, which recovers fuel costs.

Alliant lists its residential basic service charge at $0.5096 a day, about $15.50 a month.

## MidAmerican: Increase Proposed

On June 2, 2026, the Iowa Utilities Commission approved the customer notices MidAmerican must send before filing a rate case (docket RPU-2026-0001). Under Iowa law, temporary rates can start 10 days after a utility files and are subject to refund with interest. Comment meetings were set for September 8 to 10 in Cedar Rapids, Des Moines, Sioux City and online.

The commission's release does not state the size of the increase, and we did not find it elsewhere.

MidAmerican says Iowa's residential electric rates rank sixth lowest in the country. A local TV report said Alliant's residential rates were 62% higher than MidAmerican's in 2024.

## What This Means for Homeowners With Solar

Iowa law (Iowa Code 476.49) keeps solar credits at the retail rate for now, with a change scheduled.

- **Two billing methods:** utilities offer either net billing, with kWh credits at the retail rate, or inflow-outflow billing, with dollar credits measured over short intervals.
- **MidAmerican:** uses inflow-outflow. Power sent to the grid is credited at the retail volumetric rate including riders, measured in 15-minute increments.
- **Rate lock:** the outflow rate that applies when you install is fixed for 20 years, even if the rules change later.
- **Size limit:** up to 1 megawatt, and no more than 110% of your annual use.
- **What changes:** the Iowa Utilities Commission must develop a "value of solar" method when distributed generation reaches 5% statewide or by July 1, 2027, whichever is first. After that, the outflow rate for new customers can adjust each year, by no more than 5% up or down.

**What it means:** a system installed before the value-of-solar rate takes effect locks in today's retail-rate credit for 20 years. That makes timing matter more in Iowa than in most states.

Rules checked October 5, 2026. Solar tariffs change, so confirm the current terms with your utility before you sign a contract. Our [solar payback guide](/posts/solar-payback-period-without-federal-tax-credit/) shows how the retail rate and the export credit change the math.

## What to Check on Your Bill

1. The basic service charge.
2. The Energy Adjustment Clause.
3. The Regional Transmission Service line.
4. MidAmerican customers: any line labeled temporary or interim rates.

## Limitations

Alliant information comes from its own customer outlook page. We did not find MidAmerican's proposed amount, its filing date or whether temporary rates have started. We did not open commission dockets.

## Compare With Nearby States

**Statewide average for context:** the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) puts Iowa's average residential price at 15.99¢/kWh in July 2026, up 4.4% from 15.31¢ in July 2025. The U.S. average was 18.31¢. At 1,000 kWh that works out to $159.90 (1,000 × 15.99¢). This is total residential revenue divided by kWh sold across every utility in the state, fixed charges included, and it is a preliminary estimate. It is not any one utility's rate.
