**A 100-light LED mini string costs about 16 cents a month to run 6 hours a night, and a 100-light incandescent string about $1.33, at the U.S. average residential electricity price of 18.16 cents per kWh for August 2026.** Big C9 incandescent bulbs cost far more: about $5.72 a month for a 25-bulb string. Your cost depends on the wattage on the label, how many hours the lights are on and what you pay per kWh.

This guide shows the formula, works through common light strings at the U.S. average and at low and high state rates, and explains why a timer matters as much as the bulbs. For background on the units, see our explainer on [kW vs kWh](/posts/kw-vs-kwh/).

## The Formula

Your utility bills you for kilowatt-hours (kWh). A kWh is 1,000 watts used for one hour.

**Monthly cost = (watts ÷ 1,000) × hours a night × nights × your rate per kWh**

Assumptions for every example below, unless stated:

- **Rate:** 18.16 cents per kWh, EIA's U.S. average residential price for August 2026 ([Electricity Monthly Update](https://www.eia.gov/electricity/monthly/update/end-use.php), released Oct. 23, 2026).
- **Schedule:** 6 hours a night for 30 nights, or 180 hours.
- **Wattage:** the rating on specific products listed for sale, checked Nov. 8, 2026. Your lights may differ, so check the tag near the plug.

## What Common Light Strings Cost

| Light string (product rating) | Watts | kWh in 180 hours | Cost a month at 18.16¢ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 incandescent mini lights | 40.8 | 7.34 | $1.33 |
| 100 LED mini lights | 4.8 | 0.86 | $0.16 |
| 25 C9 incandescent bulbs (7 W each) | 175 | 31.5 | $5.72 |
| 25 C9 LED bulbs (0.6 W each) | 15 | 2.7 | $0.49 |

The math for the incandescent mini string: 40.8 watts ÷ 1,000 = 0.0408 kW. Then 0.0408 × 180 hours = 7.34 kWh, and 7.34 × $0.1816 = $1.33. For the C9 string: 25 bulbs × 7 watts = 175 watts, so 0.175 kW × 180 hours = 31.5 kWh, or $5.72.

Wattage sources: a 100-count clear incandescent mini set listed at 40.8 watts (Bulb America); a 100-count red LED mini set listed at 4.8 watts, 0.04 amps, 120 volts (Christmas Central); 7-watt C9 incandescent bulbs rated at 130 volts (1000Bulbs); and 0.6-watt Bulbrite C9 LED bulbs, which the listing compares to a 5-watt incandescent (SupplyStop). These are examples, not averages for all products. The C9 rows assume a 25-socket string. Because the 7-watt C9 rating is at 130 volts, these bulbs draw somewhat less on a standard 120-volt circuit, so the C9 incandescent figures are a slight overestimate.

## A Whole-House Display

Most displays use more than one string. With 10 strings on the same 6-hours-a-night schedule:

| Display | Watts | kWh in 30 nights | Cost a month at 18.16¢ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 incandescent mini strings | 408 | 73.4 | $13.34 |
| 10 LED mini strings | 48 | 8.6 | $1.57 |

If you leave the lights up from late November to early January, about 40 nights, multiply the monthly figure by about 1.33. Ten incandescent strings would come to about $17.78 for the season and ten LED strings about $2.09.

Inflatables, projectors and animated displays have their own motors and lights. Check each item's label for watts and add them in the same way.

## LED or Incandescent?

LEDs win on electricity. In our examples, the LED mini string draws 4.8 watts against 40.8 watts for the incandescent string, about 88% less. ENERGY STAR says certified decorative light strings "consume 92% less energy than conventional incandescent lights strands" and "can last up to 10 times longer than traditional incandescent strands."

The dollar savings per string are small for mini lights, about $1.18 a month at the U.S. average in our example. They add up with many strings, big C9 or C7 bulbs, long seasons and high-rate states. Whether replacing strings that still work pays off depends on how many you run and what new strings cost.

One other difference matters in practice:

- **How many strings you can connect.** One 100-light incandescent set we checked is UL listed for up to 5 strings end to end. One 100-light LED set allows up to 43 sets, "not to exceed 210 watts." Always follow your own product's label.

## Use a Timer

Hours count as much as watts. Cutting 6 hours a night to 4 trims the cost by a third with any kind of bulb. Leaving lights on all night, 12 hours, doubles it: ten incandescent mini strings would use about 147 kWh in 30 nights, about $26.67 at the U.S. average.

A plug-in timer or smart plug turns the lights off on schedule. If you are on a [time-of-use rate](/posts/time-of-use-rates-and-solar/), evening hours may be the most expensive of the day, so a later start or earlier shutoff can save more than these averages suggest.

## What About Solar?

Holiday lights run after dark, when solar panels produce nothing. How your panels help depends on your billing rules:

- **Full net metering.** Daytime exports earn credits at about the retail rate, which can offset evening use.
- **Net billing or lower export credits.** Exports may earn less than you pay for evening power, so daytime exports may cover only part of the cost of running the lights in the evening.
- **Home battery.** A battery charged by solar during the day can run the lights at night. Ten LED mini strings on our schedule use under 0.3 kWh a night (48 watts × 6 hours = 0.288 kWh).
