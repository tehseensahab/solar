California Senate Bill 868 (SB 868), chaptered September 30, 2026, removes utility interconnection requirements for small plug-in solar devices that meet a list of safety conditions. The law stops short of a free-for-all. Devices must be 1,200 watts or less, must carry a plug-in PV certification, and the exemption is written to expire on January 1, 2030.

This article is based on the chaptered bill text and the Legislative Counsel's digest of it. Where trade-press summaries differ from the text, we follow the text.

## The Short Answer

| Question | Answer under SB 868 |
|---|---|
| Who is covered? | Customers of California's investor-owned utilities and local publicly owned utilities |
| Maximum size | 1,200 W of maximum aggregated AC output per dwelling |
| Utility approval required? | No |
| Utility fees allowed? | No fee related to the device or the electricity it feeds into the building |
| Extra equipment required by utility? | No, beyond what is built into the device |
| Registration allowed? | Yes. A utility *may* require a "simple online registration form" with address, make, model and size. It cannot make this an approval step. |
| Interconnection exemption ends | January 1, 2030 (the section is repealed on that date) |

Trade press reports an effective date of January 1, 2027. That matches the default for California laws without an urgency clause, but the statute text we reviewed does not state a start date. We treat January 1, 2027 as the expected date, not a confirmed one.

## What Counts as a "Portable Solar Generation Device"

The statute (new Public Utilities Code Section 8530) defines the term as a photovoltaic energy generation device, or combination of devices, that meets **all six** of these conditions:

1. **Size:** maximum aggregated AC output of no more than 1,200 watts per dwelling.
2. **Plug-in design:** designed to be connected to and disconnected from a building's electrical system through a receptacle (a standard wall outlet).
3. **Purpose:** intended to offset the customer's on-site electricity use.
4. **Code compliance:** meets the most recent National Electrical Code and the California Electrical Code.
5. **Certification:** certified as a plug-in photovoltaic system by Underwriters Laboratories (UL) or an equivalent nationally recognized testing laboratory.
6. **Anti-backfeed feature:** includes a feature, also certified by UL or an equivalent lab, that isolates the device from the building's wiring so it cannot send power to the grid during an outage.

A device that misses any one condition does not qualify and does not get the exemptions. Condition 6 is an anti-islanding safeguard: the purpose of such a feature is generally to keep a customer's generator from energizing utility lines that are supposed to be dead during an outage.

**AC versus DC.** Solar panels are rated in DC watts, but the cap applies to AC watts at the inverter, the box that converts DC to the AC power your outlets use. A system with more DC panel capacity than 1,200 W can still fit under the AC cap if its inverter is limited to 1,200 W AC. Whether a given product's certification covers that configuration is a question for the product's certification documents, and we have not tested any product.

## What Utilities Can and Cannot Do

Section 8531 applies to both the state's investor-owned utilities (the "electrical corporations" regulated by the CPUC) and local publicly owned utilities such as municipal utilities.

**They cannot** require a customer to:

- get the utility's approval before installing or using the device;
- pay any fee or charge related to the device or the electricity it feeds into the building;
- install additional controls or equipment beyond what is integrated in the device.

**They may** require a simple online registration giving the address, make, model and size of the device. The statute says this notification "shall not require approval."

This corrects a common shorthand. At least one trade-press summary says utilities cannot require customer permission and describes no notification step. That is accurate about permission, but the statute does leave room for a registration form.

## What Changes on January 1, 2030

Two things happen on that date:

- **The interconnection exemption is repealed.** Section 8531 says it "shall remain in effect only until January 1, 2030, and as of that date is repealed." Unless the Legislature extends or replaces it, the exemption and the limits on utility requirements end.
- **A sale restriction begins.** Section 8532 says that from January 1, 2030, a plug-in photovoltaic device that does not meet conditions 4 to 6 above (code compliance, certification and anti-backfeed isolation) may not be sold or offered for sale in California.

The text we reviewed does not say what happens to devices already installed under the exemption when it lapses. We would not assume either outcome. Anyone planning a purchase should plan for the possibility that the rules will change.

## What It Could Be Worth: An Illustration

The law does not say how much power these devices produce, and output depends on the product, the sun exposure and the climate. We therefore show a simple illustration, not a forecast.

**Assumptions:** the device's output is used entirely inside the home (no export), and each kilowatt-hour (kWh) displaces power priced at 33.61¢/kWh, which is California's statewide average residential price for July 2026 in the U.S. Energy Information Administration's (EIA) *Electric Power Monthly* (released September 24, 2026, preliminary). Your own price depends on your utility, rate plan, usage tier and the hour of day. See [how to read your electric bill](/posts/how-to-read-your-electric-bill/) and [time-of-use rates and solar](/posts/time-of-use-rates-and-solar/).

| Assumed annual output (kWh) | Value at 33.61¢/kWh |
|---|---|
| 400 | $134.44 |
| 800 | $268.88 |
| 1,200 | $403.32 |

The calculation is output × 0.3361. The outputs are round numbers chosen to show scale and are not measured results for any product. The bill's sponsor, Senator Scott Wiener's office, said in a May 19, 2026 press release that a household can save up to $450 per year depending on the model and home. That is a sponsor claim and we have not independently verified it.

**Size relative to demand.** A legislative committee analysis of the bill estimated average daily electricity use in California at about 17 kWh, or roughly 700 W of continuous demand (17 kWh ÷ 24 hours = 0.71 kW), and noted that real households vary widely. A 1,200 W device can only offset power that you are using at the moment it produces it, so a home with a small daytime load may use only part of the output.

## What the Law Does Not Settle

- **Landlords and associations.** The text we reviewed does not address lease terms, landlord consent or homeowners' association rules.
- **Export credit.** No net metering or export-credit provision appears in the bill. For how California utilities credit exports from conventional systems, see our [export credit comparison](/posts/solar-export-credit-rates-by-utility-2026/) and [net metering guide](/posts/net-metering-state-guide/).
- **Product availability.** The statute requires certification from UL or an equivalent laboratory. We have not verified which products currently hold it.
- **Stringing several devices.** Utilities and the CPUC told the Legislature they were concerned that a customer could connect several devices and exceed 1,200 W, according to a committee analysis. The final text caps output "per dwelling," but the analysis shows the enforcement question was raised.
- **Other states.** pv magazine USA reports that California is the tenth state to allow plug-in solar and that Utah's HB 340 (2025) was the first. We have not independently verified the count, and New York's bill was reported as awaiting its governor's signature as of October 1, 2026.

## Before You Buy

1. Look for the device's plug-in PV certification documents and the anti-backfeed isolation feature, not just a marketing claim.
2. Check the AC output rating, not just panel wattage.
3. Ask your utility whether it requires registration, and register if so.
4. Check your lease or association rules.
5. Look at your own rate. If your utility charges time-of-use prices, the value depends on when you use the power. See our [California rate changes](/posts/california-electricity-rate-changes-2026/) page.
