**Heat pump water heater savings come to about $550 a year for a family of four, according to ENERGY STAR, compared with a standard electric tank water heater.** That estimate assumes electricity at 14.6 cents per kWh and about 3,760 kWh saved a year. Your real savings depend on how many people use hot water, what you pay per kWh, and whether you are replacing an electric or a gas heater.

This guide shows the ENERGY STAR figures, recalculates them at different electric rates, and covers the costs and installation limits that decide whether one makes sense in your home.

## What ENERGY STAR Estimates

ENERGY STAR says a certified heat pump water heater "can save a household of 4 approximately $550 per year on its electric bills" and "more than $5,610 over the HPWH's lifetime," compared with a standard electric water heater.

| Household | kWh saved a year | Dollars saved a year | Lifetime savings | Payback |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 people | 1,880 | $270 | $2,050 | 5.5 years |
| 3 people | 2,820 | $410 | $3,830 | 3.7 years |
| 4 people | 3,760 | $550 | $5,610 | 2.7 years |

Source: [ENERGY STAR](https://www.energystar.gov/products/heat_pump_water_heaters/benefits-savings), checked Oct. 28, 2026. ENERGY STAR's assumptions: electricity at 14.6 cents per kWh, an extra $1,503 up front compared with a standard electric water heater, and a 13-year life.

A newer ENERGY STAR [fact sheet from September 2025](https://www.energystar.gov/sites/default/files/2025-09/2025%20HPWH%20Factsheet%20Update%20V2.pdf) gives somewhat higher figures, about $600 a year for a family of four and $300 for two, without stating the electricity price used. We use the page with stated assumptions so you can recalculate it.

## How It Saves Energy

A standard electric water heater makes heat with resistance elements, like a toaster. A heat pump water heater "uses electricity to move heat from one place to another instead of generating heat directly," the [U.S. Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/node/783721) explains. It pulls heat from the surrounding air with refrigerant and moves it into the tank. ENERGY STAR's 2025 fact sheet says they can be "up to 4 times more efficient than conventional electric water heaters."

Some are "hybrid" models. ENERGY STAR's buying guide says they "can switch modes to operate like a standard electric model when needed."

## Savings at Your Electric Rate

The kWh saved is the more portable number. Multiply it by your own rate. Find your price per kWh on your bill (total charges divided by kWh used gives a rough all-in figure; our guide to [reading your electric bill](/posts/how-to-read-your-electric-bill/) shows where to look).

Yearly savings by our math, using ENERGY STAR's kWh estimates:

| Your electric rate | 2 people (1,880 kWh) | 4 people (3,760 kWh) |
|---|---|---|
| 10¢ per kWh | $188 | $376 |
| 15¢ per kWh | $282 | $564 |
| 20¢ per kWh | $376 | $752 |
| 30¢ per kWh | $564 | $1,128 |

These rates are examples, not any utility's tariff. Using an all-in rate slightly overstates savings, because fixed monthly charges do not fall when you use less.

**Payback by our math.** Using ENERGY STAR's $1,503 extra up-front cost, a family of four paying 15¢ would recover it in about 2.7 years ($1,503 ÷ $564). A two-person household paying 10¢ would take about 8 years ($1,503 ÷ $188). Your installer's quote, including any electrical work, replaces ENERGY STAR's assumption.

## When the Savings Shrink

- **Small households.** Less hot water means fewer kWh to save. Two people save half what four do in ENERGY STAR's table.
- **Low electric rates.** Each kWh saved is worth less.
- **Replacing gas, not electric.** ENERGY STAR's estimates compare against an electric tank. Against a gas heater, savings depend on your gas and electric prices, and the buying guide warns that "electric panel upgrades may be needed if replacing a gas water heater."
- **Cold installation spots.** ENERGY STAR says the units "perform best in spaces that remain between 40º–90ºF." A garage or basement that often gets colder than that is a poorer fit.
- **Where the cool air goes.** The unit "creates a small amount of cool, dry air." ENERGY STAR says that makes it useful in damp basements and hot garages, and the air can be ducted outside or to other rooms. Think about where that cool air will end up in winter.

## Installation Requirements

From ENERGY STAR's [buying guide](https://www.energystar.gov/sites/default/files/tools/HPWH_BuyingGuide_May2021.pdf):

- **Air volume:** "Most models need about 750 to 1000 cubic feet of air, which is about the size of a 10 ft x 10 ft room." A closet can work with a louvered door or jumper duct.
- **Power:** the most common type "requires a dedicated 240 Volt outlet."
- **Drain:** condensate goes to an existing drain or a condensate pump.
- **Noise:** "A low soft humming sound is normal."

Check the model's dimensions and clearances in its installation manual against your space before you buy.

## Tax Credits and Rebates in 2026

The federal **energy efficient home improvement credit (25C)** used to cover 30% of the cost of a heat pump water heater, up to $2,000 a year. IRS instructions for [Form 5695](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i5695) say it does not apply to property "placed in service after December 31, 2025." Materials that still mention the credit, including ENERGY STAR's 2025 fact sheet, predate that cutoff.

What may still be available: rebates from your electric utility and state programs. Amounts and eligibility vary by utility and change often, so check your utility's rebate page and your state energy office before you buy, and confirm whether the rebate needs pre-approval.

**A federal standard is coming.** The Department of Energy's 2024 water heater rule requires compliance "on and after May 6, 2029." For electric storage water heaters, the rule's standards for larger products "are met through the use of heat pump technology," according to the [Federal Register notice](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-05-06/html/2024-09209.htm). The rule covers new products made or imported from that date, not water heaters already in homes.

## Heat Pump Water Heaters and Solar

In a home with an electric tank, a heat pump water heater can cut a meaningful share of electric use. For a family of four, ENERGY STAR's 3,760 kWh a year is about 313 kWh a month. By our math, the 3,760 kWh a year is roughly the yearly output of 2.7 kW of solar panels at the U.S. mean output of about 1,375 kWh per kW used in our [solar system size calculator](/solar-system-size-calculator/). Cutting that load before you size a system can mean fewer panels.

If you already have solar, timing can matter. ENERGY STAR's 2025 fact sheet says many units include connectivity; if yours can be scheduled, heating the tank midday, when panels are producing, uses more of your own solar. Where exported solar earns less than the retail rate, using more of your own solar at home saves more. Our guide to [time-of-use rates and solar](/posts/time-of-use-rates-and-solar/) explains why.

## How to Decide

1. **Check your current heater's fuel and age.** Savings figures above assume replacing an electric tank.
2. **Find your rate and household size** and read your savings from the table.
3. **Measure the space** for air volume, height and temperature.
4. **Ask for quotes** that include the unit, installation, any 240-volt circuit or panel work, and the condensate drain.
5. **Subtract rebates** that you have confirmed with your utility or state.
6. **Divide the extra cost by yearly savings** to get your payback.

If you are also weighing a heat pump for space heating, our [guide to choosing a heat pump](/posts/how-to-choose-a-heat-pump/) covers sizing and efficiency ratings.

## Limitations

ENERGY STAR's figures are national estimates, not measurements from your home. We did not research gas-to-electric savings, state rebate amounts, or specific models and prices. Savings tables at other electric rates are our calculations from ENERGY STAR's kWh estimates.
