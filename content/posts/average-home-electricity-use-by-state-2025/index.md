The average U.S. home used 874 kilowatt-hours (kWh) of electricity a month in 2025 and paid an average of $150.68 a month, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA). A kilowatt-hour is the unit utilities bill for: running a 1,000-watt appliance for one hour uses 1 kWh.

These are final figures for calendar year 2025 from EIA's Form EIA-861 survey of electric utilities, released on October 7, 2026. They are not the preliminary monthly estimates EIA publishes during the year. The numbers are averages across every residential customer in the country, so they describe a benchmark, not your home.

## The Short Version

| Measure (all U.S. residential customers) | 2024 | 2025 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Average monthly use | 863.28 kWh | 874.28 kWh | +1.3% |
| Average price | 16.48¢/kWh | 17.23¢/kWh | +4.6% |
| Average monthly bill | $142.26 | $150.68 | +5.9% |
| Residential customers | 143,144,185 | 144,800,940 | +1.2% |

**How the pieces fit:** average monthly bill = average monthly use × average price. For 2025: 874.28 kWh × 17.2346¢ (EIA's unrounded price) = $150.68. Price rose 4.6% and use rose 1.3%, and 1.046 × 1.013 ≈ 1.059, which is the 5.9% increase in the bill.

**Over a year:** 874.28 kWh × 12 months = 10,491 kWh.

## What "Average" Means Here

Read these limits before using the numbers:

- **It is a mean, not a typical home.** EIA divides total residential sales by the number of residential customers. A few very large users raise it, so many households use less than 874 kWh.
- **Customers are utility accounts.** EIA's form instructions say to report the average of the 12 month-end customer account counts. The table does not count people or housing units.
- **The price is revenue per kWh.** EIA's instructions say the revenue includes energy and demand charges, customer service charges, environmental surcharges, franchise fees, fuel adjustments and state and local taxes the utility reports. So the "price" includes fixed charges and is higher than the per-kWh rate on a tariff.
- **It covers the whole year.** The figure averages hot and cold months. Your summer and winter bills will differ from it. See our [winter heating forecast](/posts/winter-heating-costs-2026-27/) for the seasonal side.
- **It is a state-level blend.** Each state figure combines every utility, home type and rate plan in the state.

Seventeen states had average use of 1,000 kWh a month or more. Louisiana's average is 2.5 times California's (1,224.85 ÷ 493.53 = 2.48).

EIA's table does not say why use differs. It reports totals, not heating, cooling or home size, so we do not attribute the gaps to a cause.

## Two Routes to a High Bill: Use and Price

A bill is use multiplied by price, so a high bill can come from either.

- **High price, modest use: Connecticut.** Its average customer used 704.34 kWh, below the U.S. average of 874.28 kWh, but paid 29.31¢ per kWh, about 70% above the U.S. average of 17.23¢. The result was $206.46 a month.
- **High use, moderate price: Alabama.** Its average customer used 1,145.43 kWh, about 31% more than the U.S. average, at 16.09¢ per kWh, which is below the national average. The result was $184.31.
- **Both effects can cancel out: Louisiana and California.**

| 2025 | Use (kWh/month) | Price (¢/kWh) | Average bill |
|---|---|---|---|
| Louisiana | 1,224.85 | 12.52 | $153.32 |
| California | 493.53 | 31.35 | $154.73 |

Louisiana used 2.48 times as much electricity as California. California's price was 2.50 times Louisiana's (31.35 ÷ 12.52). The two bills differ by $1.41. A state's rank for price alone, or for use alone, tells you little about what its households pay. Our [July 2026 price comparison](/posts/electricity-prices-by-state-july-2026/) covers prices; that is a different measure (one summer month, preliminary) from the full-year 2025 figures here, so the two should not be compared directly.

## Using This If You Are Thinking About Solar

Your own bills are a better starting point than the state average, but the benchmark helps you spot whether a quote is sized for a much larger or smaller home than yours.

**The sizing arithmetic:** annual use in kWh ÷ expected annual output per kW of panels = system size in kW. The output per kW depends on your location, roof direction, tilt and shading, so get it from a site-specific tool or your installer's production estimate.

**Illustration only.** Suppose 1 kW of panels produced 1,300 kWh a year at your site. This is an assumed number for the arithmetic, not a measured result or a statewide figure.

| Annual use to cover | Annual kWh | Size at the assumed 1,300 kWh per kW |
|---|---|---|
| U.S. average (874.28 kWh × 12) | 10,491 | 8.07 kW |
| Louisiana average (1,224.85 × 12) | 14,698 | 11.31 kW |
| California average (493.53 × 12) | 5,922 | 4.56 kW |

For context on how common net-metered solar already is in each state, see our [state-by-state count from EIA](/posts/net-metered-home-solar-by-state-2025/). Covering 100% of use is a design choice, not a requirement. Whether it pays depends on what your utility credits for power you export, which varies widely. Our [solar system size calculator](/solar-system-size-calculator/) walks through the sizing, and the [solar payback guide](/posts/solar-payback-period-without-federal-tax-credit/) shows how price and export credits change the result. Fixed charges stay even if your kWh fall; see [fixed charges and solar savings](/posts/fixed-charges-and-solar-savings/).

## How to Compare Your Own Home

1. **Find 12 months of use.** Add the kWh from your last 12 bills, or download the data from your utility account. Divide by 12.
2. **Compute your price.** Divide your total electric charges for those 12 months by the total kWh. That includes your fixed charges, so it matches what EIA's average represents. Our guide to [reading your electric bill](/posts/how-to-read-your-electric-bill/) shows where each line sits.
3. **Compare like with like.** Compare your monthly average use with your state's figure in the table, and your price with the state price. If your use is far above the state average, the cause is in your home or its heating and cooling, not in the state's rates.
4. **If your bill swings widely by season**, a levelized plan can smooth payments but does not lower them. See [budget billing explained](/posts/budget-billing-explained/).

## Limitations

- These are 2025 data. Use and prices in 2026 may differ. EIA's monthly estimates for 2026 are preliminary and are not comparable to these final annual figures.
- The 2024 comparison uses EIA's Table 5A as published on October 7, 2025. EIA can revise earlier years, and we did not check whether it has revised its 2024 values since.
- Averages hide wide spreads within every state. Your own home can sit far from the average.
- The sizing example uses an assumed output per kW and is not a quote or a forecast.
- EIA's 2025 download includes form instructions labeled 2024. We used them only for the definitions of customers and revenue, and confirmed the same wording in EIA's current instructions.
