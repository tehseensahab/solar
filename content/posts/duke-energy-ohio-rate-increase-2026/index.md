Duke Energy Ohio has asked state regulators to raise the delivery part of its electric rates, an increase the company says would add about **$8 a month** for a household using 1,000 kWh, about 4.5% of the total bill. Nothing is approved. The Public Utilities Commission of Ohio (PUCO) has set three public hearings for **December 1 and December 10, 2026**, and the state's residential consumer advocate has intervened to challenge it.

The application is Case No. 26-0132-EL-AIR. It covers Duke's electric distribution charges in southwest Ohio, the Cincinnati area, and not the supply price, other Ohio utilities, or Duke's separate natural gas case. Our [Ohio electric rates and solar page](/rates/ohio/) covers default supply prices for AEP Ohio, AES Ohio and the FirstEnergy utilities.

## The Request at a Glance

| Item | Figure | Status / source |
|---|---|---|
| What changes | Electric distribution (delivery) charges | Requested; OCC fact sheet |
| Residential bill at 1,000 kWh | About +$8 a month, about 4.5% of the total bill | Duke estimate, requested (Duke spokesperson to WKRC/Local 12 and FOX19, June 2026) |
| Typical residential bill | About +$8.32 a month | OCC fact sheet, requested |
| Distribution charges alone | About 38% increase | OCC, requested |
| Non-residential customers | About 3% | Duke estimate (FOX19), requested |
| Total revenue requested | Not found in the sources we could check | — |
| Earliest change | 2027, if approved | OCC fact sheet |

Duke's dollar estimate and OCC's are close: about $8.00 versus $8.32 a month. They diverge on percentages because they measure different things. Duke's 4.5% is a share of the whole bill, which includes supply and transmission. OCC's 38% is the change to distribution charges alone, which OCC says it focuses on because "Duke only has authority over the distribution charges," according to WKRC/Local 12.

Local 12 also reported an OCC consumer alert that put the rise in distribution charges at "nearly $18 per month" for a typical customer. That is a distribution-only measure, unlike the $8.32 total-bill figure on OCC's fact sheet. Duke's response, as reported by Local 12, is that OCC's distribution-only figure ignores distribution riders that would decrease or be rolled into base rates under the proposal. We could not find that alert, so treat the $18 figure as OCC's estimate as reported, not as a confirmed bill impact.

## Why Duke Says It Needs the Increase

Duke told FOX19 in June that the request covers infrastructure modernization and capital investments made since its electric distribution rates were last raised in 2021. Duke told FOX19 this month that "There is never a good time to propose a rate increase" and that "at the end of the day, our job is to keep the lights on."

Duke told FOX19 in June that it filed at the end of March 2026, though a FOX19 story this month says June. The trade publication EnergyChoiceMatters reported on September 21 that PUCO staff had filed its report in the case. We were not able to obtain the staff report itself, so we cannot say what revenue increase, if any, staff recommends.

OCC has intervened on behalf of about 500,000 Duke residential customers and says it will present evidence on affordability. It also plans to ask the PUCO for a virtual public hearing; none had been scheduled as of OCC's fact sheet.

## What It Means for Your Bill

A distribution case changes the delivery side of your bill: the fixed monthly customer charge and per-kWh delivery charges. It does not change the price of the electricity itself. If you buy supply from a competitive supplier or through a city aggregation program, your supply price follows your contract, but you still pay Duke's distribution rates.

Using Duke's own figure, the request works out to about **$96 a year** for a 1,000 kWh-a-month home ($8 × 12). OCC's typical-bill figure works out to about **$100 a year** ($8.32 × 12); OCC does not state the usage behind it. Your own change depends on how much you use and on how the PUCO splits any increase between fixed and per-kWh charges. We could not find that proposed split in the sources we checked. Our guide to [reading your electric bill](/posts/how-to-read-your-electric-bill/) shows where distribution charges and riders appear on a statement.

## What It Means if You Have or Want Solar

- **Solar that offsets your own use:** every kWh your panels supply to your home avoids Duke's per-kWh distribution charge. Under Ohio net metering, imports and exports are netted over each billing period, so exports that offset same-month use count too. A higher per-kWh distribution rate makes that solar slightly more valuable.
- **Net excess:** only the kWh left over after a month's netting are credited, and at the energy portion of the supply rate alone, not delivery, so that credit would not gain from this case. See our [rundown of Ohio net metering in 2026](/posts/ohio-net-metering-rule-review-2026/).
- **Fixed charges:** any increase to the monthly customer charge is billed whether or not you have solar. Our explainer on [fixed charges and solar savings](/posts/fixed-charges-and-solar-savings/) shows why that matters for payback.

If you are comparing solar quotes in Duke's territory, ask installers which rate they assumed for savings. A quote built on a distribution rate that has not been approved is a forecast, not a fact.

## What Happens Next

1. Local public hearings on December 1 and December 10, 2026.
2. An evidentiary hearing before the PUCO, where Duke, PUCO staff, OCC and other parties present testimony. Parties may also negotiate a settlement, which the PUCO would still have to approve.
3. A PUCO order. OCC says an approved increase would take effect in 2027; Duke told FOX19 it expected the following May.

OCC also lists a separate Duke natural gas distribution case (Case No. 26-0635-GA-AIR), which this article does not cover.
