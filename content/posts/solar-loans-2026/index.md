**A solar loan can cost much more than its rate suggests. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) found that lenders often add "dealer fees" of 10% to 30% of the cash price, sometimes more than 50%, to the loan amount, and leave them out of the APR shown to borrowers.** In 2026 there is a second risk: many solar loans were designed around a prepayment equal to the 30% federal tax credit, and the IRS says that credit is not allowed for home systems installed after December 31, 2025.

This guide shows what a dealer fee costs in dollars, how the prepayment clause works, and what to ask before you sign. For the bigger choice between paying cash, borrowing, leasing or signing a power purchase agreement, see our guide to [solar lease vs. buying in 2026](/posts/solar-lease-vs-buy/). For what a system should cost before financing, see [how much solar panels cost](/posts/how-much-do-solar-panels-cost/).

## What Is a Dealer Fee on a Solar Loan?

A dealer fee is a markup built into the loan principal on top of the price you would pay in cash. The CFPB's August 2024 report on solar financing says "hidden fees typically range from between 10 to 30 percent of the cash price but can exceed 50 percent." Its press release adds that these fees "often increase the loan cost by 30% or more above the cash price of a solar project."

The fee is easy to miss because of how it is shown. The CFPB found that "typically, lenders do not include the hidden fees in the total costs of credit that they present to consumers," and that "salespeople offering financing often do not explain the difference in the cash price and loan principal." Its consumer advisory says the fees "are rarely shown to the customer" and "make the interest rate on the loan look artificially low."

## How Much Does a Dealer Fee Cost?

The CFPB's example is a system "that would cost $30,000 if the consumer paid in cash." The loan documents show "a loan principal of $39,000 plus interest," and the lender keeps the $9,000 difference. Here is what that does to the payments:

| Scenario | Amount borrowed | APR | Term | Monthly payment | Total of payments |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Loan with the $9,000 dealer fee | $39,000 | 2.99% | 25 years | $184.74 | $55,422 |
| Same rate, no dealer fee | $30,000 | 2.99% | 25 years | $142.11 | $42,632 |
| Difference | $9,000 | | | $42.63 a month | $12,790 |

**Assumptions:** cash price and loan amount from the CFPB example; 2.99% APR and 25 years are our illustrative choices within the ranges the CFPB reports (stated APRs "typically range from 1 to 7 percent" and terms "from 8 to 25 years"). Payments use the standard fixed-rate loan formula with monthly payments; totals assume every payment is made for the full term with no prepayment. The CFPB notes that solar loans are "typically repaid in 7 to 9 years due to prepayments," so most borrowers would pay less interest than the full-term totals.

Measured against the $30,000 you actually received in panels and installation, the $184.74 payment works out to an effective rate of about 5.5%, not 2.99%. That is our calculation: the interest rate at which $30,000 over 25 years gives the same payment. It is why a "low-rate" loan with a fee can cost more than a higher-rate loan without one.

## What Is the Tax-Credit Prepayment on a Solar Loan?

It is a large payment the loan expects you to make early, frequently 30% of the loan principal, the size of the federal tax credit you may have been told to expect. The CFPB found it "commonplace for solar-specific loans to re-amortize at a higher monthly payment amount at the 19th month of the loan term if the consumer does not make a substantial prepayment before then," and that "frequently, the necessary prepayment to avoid that re-amortization is 30 percent of the loan principal, which is the current size of the federal Investment Tax Credit." The CFPB's press release adds that lenders "present loan principals as a 'net cost' that assume the tax credit will be received."

Here is how that could play out on the $39,000 loan above, as an illustration. Loan terms vary, so check your own agreement:

| Step | Amount |
|---|---|
| Expected prepayment (30% of $39,000) | $11,700 |
| Payment for months 1 to 18, set as if the $11,700 will be repaid ($27,300 over 25 years at 2.99%) | $129.32 |
| Balance after 18 payments | About $38,409 |
| New payment from month 19 if no prepayment (balance over the remaining 282 months) | About $189.77 |
| New payment from month 19 if the $11,700 is paid | About $131.97 |

**Assumptions:** our illustration of one common structure, using the same 2.99% APR and 25-year term. Your lender's method, deadline and new payment may differ.

## Why the Prepayment Is a Problem in 2026

The homeowner tax credit behind those prepayments is gone for new installations. The IRS says the Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) "will not be allowed for any expenditures made after December 31, 2025," and that "an expenditure with respect to an item is treated as made when the original installation of the item is completed." Our [payback guide without the federal tax credit](/posts/solar-payback-period-without-federal-tax-credit/) shows what that does to the math.

What it means for you:

- **Buying and installing in 2026.** You cannot claim Section 25D for the system, so a loan whose payment assumes a 30% prepayment has no federal tax credit to help fund it. If you are shown a "net cost" after a federal credit, ask what credit, and get the answer in writing.
- **Signed or paid in 2025, installed in 2026.** Per the IRS rule above, the credit depends on when installation was completed, not when you signed or paid. If your loan assumed a credit you will not receive, read the prepayment terms now and talk to your lender before the deadline.
- **Installed by the end of 2025.** You may be eligible for the credit if you meet the other requirements, but the CFPB warns "the projected tax credit might not be accurate and the actual tax credit might take time to arrive." The credit also reduces taxes you owe; ask a tax professional how much you can actually use.
- **Leasing or signing a power purchase agreement (PPA).** These are different contracts, with no loan to re-amortize. Our [lease vs. buy guide](/posts/solar-lease-vs-buy/) covers how they are priced in 2026.

## Questions to Ask Before You Sign a Solar Loan

1. **What is the cash price of this exact system?** The CFPB advises homeowners to "ask the salesperson what the cash price of the system is." Get it in writing.
2. **Is the loan amount higher than the cash price?** If so, the difference is a fee you borrow and pay interest on.
3. **What is the APR, and does it include every fee?** The CFPB found APRs "often do not include fees that increase the loan principal on top of the cash price."
4. **Is there a prepayment I am expected to make, how much, and by when?** Ask what the payment becomes if you do not make it.
5. **Does any number assume a federal tax credit?** For a system installed in 2026, the homeowner credit is not allowed.
6. **Will the lender file a lien?** The CFPB notes some solar loans are "secured by collateral (typically with a lien on the panels themselves)." A report it cites says such a lien is "technically not on the property, but it can muddy the title," and may need to be released or made subordinate to a new mortgage or refinance, including a buyer's mortgage when you sell.
7. **What would my bank or credit union charge?** The CFPB suggests you "consider going to a bank or credit union for financing, and bring that loan offer to the installer," and "shop around and compare proposals from multiple installers and lenders." Our guide on [how to compare solar quotes](/posts/how-to-compare-solar-quotes/) has a checklist.

## Can You Cancel a Solar Contract Signed at Home?

Often, but only for a short window. The Federal Trade Commission's Cooling-Off Rule "gives you three days to cancel certain sales made at your home, workplace, or dormitory," including when "you invite a salesperson to make a presentation in your home." Your "right to cancel for a full refund lasts until midnight of the third business day after the sale." Saturdays count as business days; Sundays and federal holidays do not.

To cancel, sign and date the cancellation form and mail it so it is postmarked before the deadline, or send your own letter if you were not given a form. The FTC suggests certified mail. The rule does not cover sales under $25 at your home, or sales involving real estate, insurance or securities, and your state may have its own rules. If you are unsure whether it applies to your contract, ask your state attorney general's consumer office or a lawyer.

**Who this is for:** homeowners being offered installer-arranged solar financing, and borrowers who signed a solar loan expecting a federal tax credit. It does not cover leases, PPAs or community solar subscriptions in detail.
