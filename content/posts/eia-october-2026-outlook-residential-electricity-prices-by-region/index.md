The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) projects that the average American household will pay 18.21 cents per kilowatt-hour (kWh) for electricity in 2026 and 18.68 cents in 2027, according to its Short-Term Energy Outlook released October 6, 2026. That compares with 17.30 cents in 2025. By our calculation from EIA's table, that is an increase of 5.3% in 2026 and a further 2.6% in 2027.

The national average hides large regional gaps. EIA's outlook includes prices for nine groups of states, called Census divisions. Here is how they compare.

## Residential Prices by Region

EIA reports average residential price in cents per kWh, in nominal dollars (not adjusted for inflation). The 2025 column is EIA's historical figure. The 2026 and 2027 columns are projections, and 2026 combines reported data with forecast. EIA completed this forecast on October 1, 2026.

| East North Central | IL, IN, MI, OH, WI | 17.70 | 19.27 | 19.98 | +8.9% | +3.7% | +0.27¢ |

Prices are cents per kWh. Percent changes and revisions are our calculations from EIA Table 7c, using the September 2026 edition's figures for the revision column. The U.S. average is weighted by sales, so it is not a simple average of the nine divisions.

## What Stands Out

**The biggest 2026 increases are in the Mid-Atlantic and Great Lakes states.** The Middle Atlantic division rose 2.20 cents (+9.7%) from 2025 to 2026. The East North Central rose 1.57 cents (+8.9%). EIA's outlook text says wholesale prices in the PJM region (a regional grid operator) are forecast to rise 41% in 2026 compared with 2025, the largest increase of any region it tracks. EIA does not tie that figure to the division prices above. Wholesale price is only one component. EIA says residential prices also include transmission and distribution costs and, in some regions, capacity market auction prices. We are not attributing the division figures to any single cause.

**New England is flat in 2026 but still the most expensive.** At 29.06 cents in 2026, it is about twice the 14.44 cents projected for the West North Central division, the lowest. EIA projects New England prices to rise 3.1% in 2027.

**2027 growth slows nationally.** EIA expects wholesale prices to decline slightly, from an average of $52 per megawatt-hour in 2026 to $49 in 2027, across the 11 trading hubs it tracks. It also expects residential electricity consumption to remain mostly unchanged from 2026. Residential retail prices are still projected to rise 2.6%.

**The 2027 forecast moved up slightly in October.** The U.S. residential average for 2027 rose from 18.59 cents in the September outlook to 18.68 cents. Two divisions were revised down for 2027, the West South Central (−0.02 cents) and the Mountain (−0.06 cents). These are small revisions in a forecast, not new price data.

## Rooftop Solar in the Same Outlook

EIA's table of electric power capacity also covers small-scale solar, meaning systems under one megawatt, which includes home rooftop systems. For the residential sector, EIA shows 40.5 gigawatts (GW) of capacity at the end of 2025, 44.2 GW at the end of 2026 and 48.0 GW at the end of 2027. That is an addition of about 3.7 GW in 2026 and 3.8 GW in 2027, by our subtraction.

In the September edition, the corresponding end-of-year figures were 44.5 GW for 2026 and 48.4 GW for 2027, so EIA trimmed those projections by 0.3 GW and 0.4 GW. EIA's tables do not say why. Our [guide to who can claim solar tax credits in 2026](/posts/solar-lease-ppa-tax-credit-2026/) covers the federal credit that ended for homeowner-owned systems placed in service after December 31, 2025, but we cannot say from EIA's tables whether that explains the revision.

## What Higher Prices Mean for Solar Savings

Higher prices raise the value of each kWh a solar system lets you avoid buying. The effect on your savings depends on how your utility credits what you send to the grid.

- **Power you use as it is produced** avoids your retail rate, so a higher rate raises its value directly.
- **Power you export** earns your utility's export credit, which in many places is lower than the retail rate and may not rise with it. See our comparison of [solar export credit rates by utility](/posts/solar-export-credit-rates-by-utility-2026/).

A regional average is a poor input for a savings estimate. Use the rate on your own bill, as explained in our [guide to reading your electric bill](/posts/how-to-read-your-electric-bill/). For actual state-level prices, see our [state electricity price data for July 2026](/posts/electricity-prices-by-state-july-2026/) and our earlier look at [EIA's September forecast](/posts/eia-residential-price-forecast-sept-2026/).

## Limits of This Data

- **Forecasts change.** EIA revises each month, and the next outlook is scheduled for November 10, 2026.
- **Regions, not states.** The outlook gives nine multi-state divisions. Individual states in a division can move very differently.
- **Averages, not rates.** The figures are average revenue per kWh. They include fixed charges and blend all utilities and rate plans in a division.
- **Nominal dollars.** They are not adjusted for inflation.
