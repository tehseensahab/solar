**A U.S. home with a clothes washer used an average of 69 kWh of electricity a year on it in 2020, according to the Energy Information Administration (EIA). At the U.S. average residential price of 18.16 cents per kWh for August 2026, that is about $12.53 a year, or $1.04 a month.** A load costs roughly 4 to 5 cents for the machine itself, based on EIA's average; a model's EnergyGuide rating, which also counts water heating with an electric water heater, can run higher. The bigger cost is hot water: ENERGY STAR says heating water takes about 90% of the energy a washer uses.

On EIA's figures, the washer itself costs far less to run than the dryer next to it: a [clothes dryer](/posts/dryer-cost-to-run/) uses about ten times as much electricity, and an [electric water heater](/posts/electric-water-heater-cost-to-run/) may carry most of the cost of a warm or hot wash. This guide shows the math per load, month and year, by household size and by state. To use your own price, look it up on our [electricity rates by state](/rates-by-state/) page.

## The Formula

**Cost = kWh used × your price per kWh**

Your price per kWh is on your bill. If your bill splits supply and delivery charges, add the per-kWh parts together and leave out the fixed monthly customer charge. Our [kW vs kWh explainer](/posts/kw-vs-kwh/) covers the units.

## How Much Does It Cost to Run a Washing Machine per Load?

About 4 to 5 cents for the machine alone at 18.16 cents per kWh, using EIA's average. The EnergyGuide rows also include the energy an electric water heater uses for the wash. Three ways to estimate it:

| Basis | kWh a year | Loads a year | kWh a load | Cost a load at 18.16¢ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| EIA 2020 average, spread over ENERGY STAR's ~300 loads | 69 | 300 | 0.23 | About 4¢ |
| EIA 2020 average, spread over DOE's 234 test cycles | 69 | 234 | 0.29 | About 5¢ |
| Whirlpool WFW87HED front-loader, EnergyGuide label | 145 | 416 (8 a week) | 0.35 | About 6¢ |
| Lowest-use similar model on that label | 77 | 416 | 0.19 | About 3¢ |
| Highest-use similar model on that label | 504 | 416 | 1.21 | About 22¢ |

**How we got these:** kWh a load = kWh a year ÷ loads a year. Cost a load = kWh a load × $0.1816. EIA does not publish how many loads its surveyed homes ran, so the first two rows pair EIA's yearly average with two published load counts: ENERGY STAR's "about 300 loads of laundry each year" for the average family and the 234 cycles a year in the Department of Energy's (DOE) newer test procedure. The label rows use the label's "eight wash loads a week" (8 × 52 = 416).

The EnergyGuide figures are for one model, and they come from a lab test rather than real homes. The label prices its yearly cost at a 2007 national average of 10.65 cents per kWh: 145 kWh × $0.1065 is about $15.44, in line with the $15 it lists for homes with an electric water heater. At 18.16 cents the same 145 kWh is about $26.33 a year. DOE's test procedure counts the energy to heat the wash water as well as the machine's own electricity, assuming an electric water heater, so a label's kWh figure includes water heating and is not directly comparable with EIA's average for the washer itself.

## How Much Does a Washer Add to a Monthly Bill?

About $1.04 a month in an average home, for the machine alone. EIA's 69 kWh a year × $0.1816 = $12.53 a year, and $12.53 ÷ 12 = $1.04 a month.

By region, EIA's averages are close together: Northeast 64 kWh, Midwest 70, South 72 and West 65. At the U.S. average price that is $11.62 to $13.08 a year. Your state's price matters far more than your region (see the state table below).

## Washing Machine Cost by Household Size

More people means more loads. EIA's 2020 averages for homes with a clothes washer, priced at 18.16 cents per kWh:

| People in the home | kWh a year | Cost a year | Cost a month |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 46 | $8.35 | $0.70 |
| 2 | 64 | $11.62 | $0.97 |
| 3 | 77 | $13.98 | $1.17 |
| 4 | 87 | $15.80 | $1.32 |
| 5 | 98 | $17.80 | $1.48 |
| 6 or more | 111 | $20.16 | $1.68 |

These are survey estimates for the washer's own electricity. They do not tell you how much your water heater spends on laundry.

## Hot Water Is Most of the Cost

ENERGY STAR says "water heating consumes about 90% of the energy it takes to operate a clothes washer." It also says switching your temperature setting "from hot to warm can cut energy use in half," and "using the cold cycle reduces energy use even more."

What that means for your bill depends on your water heater:

- **Electric water heater.** Warm and hot washes add to the electricity your water heater uses. That cost appears in your water heater's usage, not the washer's. Our [electric water heater cost guide](/posts/electric-water-heater-cost-to-run/) shows how to price it.
- **Gas water heater.** The hot water shows up on your gas bill instead. The washer's own electricity is the same.
- **Cold-only washing.** You pay mostly for the washer's own electricity, the few cents a load shown above from EIA's average.

We did not find a federal per-load figure for the hot water a typical wash uses, so we do not estimate it here.

## Front-Load vs. Top-Load Washers

ENERGY STAR says certified front-load washers "use about 50% less energy and water than a top load agitator washer" and are "about 25% more energy and water efficient" than a top-load impeller washer. Certified washers overall "use about 20% less energy and about 30% less water than regular washers," and a full-size certified model uses 14 gallons of water a load, compared with 20 for a standard machine.

ENERGY STAR estimates certified models "can save about $530 in energy costs" over the product's lifetime. We did not find the assumptions behind that figure on the page, so treat it as a rough guide. Since the machine itself costs about $1 a month to run on EIA's average, we expect most of any saving to come from using less hot water.

ENERGY STAR's criteria for clothes washers changed on April 22, 2021. A front-loader larger than 2.5 cubic feet must score an integrated modified energy factor (IMEF) of at least 2.76 and a top-loader of that size at least 2.06. Higher IMEF means more efficient. For shopping, the simpler number is the kWh a year on the yellow EnergyGuide label.

## How to Cut Your Washing Machine's Cost

1. **Wash in cold water.** It is the biggest lever, because heating water takes about 90% of a washer's energy.
2. **Run full loads.** Fewer loads means fewer cycles and less hot water.
3. **Shift laundry off-peak if you are on time-of-use rates.** The washer itself is a small load, but the dryer and an electric water heater are not. Our guide to [time-of-use rates and solar](/posts/time-of-use-rates-and-solar/) explains peak windows.
4. **Compare EnergyGuide labels when you replace a washer.** Similar standard models on one label ranged from 77 to 504 kWh a year.

**Who this is for:** households estimating laundry costs or comparing washers. If you run a coin laundry or share a building's washers, your costs are set differently. If you have rooftop solar, daytime laundry can use your own panels' output, but what that is worth depends on your utility's export credit.

## Limitations

EIA's washer figures are from its 2020 survey and are estimates; we found no newer EIA figures for clothes washers. EIA does not report how many loads its surveyed homes ran, so our per-load figures from EIA's average depend on the load count we pair it with. Label ratings assume a set number of loads and lab test settings, and your use will differ. We could not find a federal per-load figure for the hot water a typical wash uses. State prices are averages, may not include sales taxes, and change monthly.
