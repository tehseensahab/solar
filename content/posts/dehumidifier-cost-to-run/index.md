**A portable dehumidifier costs about 7 to 11 cents an hour to run while its compressor is on.** The three models we checked are rated at 380 to 593 watts, and the Energy Information Administration's (EIA) U.S. average residential price was 18.16 cents per kWh in August 2026. Averaged over a year, households that use a dehumidifier spend about $15 a month on it, based on EIA survey data.

The bill depends far more on hours than on the model: a dehumidifier in a damp basement in July can run most of the day, while the same unit in a dry winter barely runs. This guide shows the math, what the capacity and efficiency labels mean, and how to cut the cost. If you are new to the units, [kW vs kWh](/posts/kw-vs-kwh/) explains the difference between watts and the kWh on your bill.

## The Formula

**Cost = watts ÷ 1,000 × hours the compressor runs × your price per kWh**

Use the watts on the rating label or spec sheet, not amps times volts. The two Frigidaire sheets we checked list amps that work out to more than the rated watts (5.3 amps at 120 volts is 636 volt-amperes, against 380 rated watts), so amps overstate the running cost. Your price per kWh is on your bill; add supply and delivery per-kWh charges together and leave out the fixed monthly charge.

## How Much Does a Dehumidifier Cost to Run per Hour?

At EIA's August 2026 U.S. average residential price of 18.16 cents per kWh (released Oct. 23, 2026; one month's price, not a yearly average):

| Model (manufacturer document) | Capacity (pints per day) | Rated power | Cost per hour at 18.16¢ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frigidaire FFAD3533W1 (spec sheet, version 02/23) | 35.23 | 380 watts | 6.9¢ |
| Midea Cube MAD50PS1QGRC (spec sheet, Canadian model) | 49.58 | 480 watts | 8.7¢ |
| Frigidaire FHDP5033W1, with pump (spec sheet, version 02/25) | 50 | 593 watts | 10.8¢ |

How we calculated it: watts ÷ 1,000 × $0.1816. These are three examples, not a survey of every model. The per-hour cost applies only while the compressor runs; once the room reaches your humidity setting, the compressor cycles off and the fan alone uses far less (we did not find a published fan-only wattage for these models).

## How Much Does It Cost per Day and per Month?

Hours are the big unknown. We found no official figure for typical daily run time, so the table shows two examples: 12 hours a day (a damp basement in humid weather) and 24 hours a day (the maximum).

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 380 watts (35-pint) | 4.56 | $0.83 | 136.8 | $24.84 | $1.66 |
| 480 watts (50-pint) | 5.76 | $1.05 | 172.8 | $31.38 | $2.09 |
| 593 watts (50-pint) | 7.12 | $1.29 | 213.5 | $38.77 | $2.58 |

All at 18.16 cents per kWh (our math). In California, at EIA's August 2026 state average of 29.75 cents, the 593-watt model at 12 hours a day for 30 days costs about $63.51.

### What EIA's survey says about real use

EIA's 2020 Residential Energy Consumption Survey (Table CE5.3a) puts average dehumidifier electricity use at 999 kWh a year for households that use one. At 18.16 cents per kWh, that is about $181 a year, or about $15 a month averaged over 12 months (our math). Use is uneven across the year, so humid summer months cost more than that average and dry months less.

## What Do Pints per Day and IEF Mean?

**Capacity (pints per day)** is how much water the unit removes in 24 hours under the federal test. Since June 2019, the U.S. Department of Energy test runs portable dehumidifiers at 65°F rather than 80°F. "There's less water to remove in cooler air, which is why the capacities have decreased," ENERGY STAR explains. Its conversion table, which it calls approximate, puts an old 70-pint model at roughly 40 to 45 pints under the new test. If you are replacing an older unit, compare like with like.

**Integrated energy factor (IEF)** is liters of water removed per kWh, and it counts the energy the unit uses after it cycles off. Higher is better. The minimums:

| Portable capacity (pints per day) | Federal minimum IEF (since June 13, 2019) | ENERGY STAR minimum IEF (Version 6.0, Oct. 1, 2025) |
|---|---|---|
| 25.00 or less | 1.30 L/kWh | 1.70 L/kWh |
| 25.01 to 50.00 | 1.60 L/kWh | 2.01 L/kWh |
| 50.01 or more | 2.80 L/kWh | 3.30 L/kWh |

### What efficiency is worth in dollars

Removing 50 pints of water (about 23.66 liters) takes about 14.8 kWh at the federal minimum of 1.60 L/kWh and about 11.8 kWh at the ENERGY STAR minimum of 2.01 L/kWh. At 18.16 cents, that is about $2.69 versus $2.14 for the same water removed (our math). ENERGY STAR describes the gap as certified models being "20% more efficient than non-certified models." That works out to about 17% less electricity for the same water removed; on EIA's 999 kWh annual average, about 167 kWh, or about $30 a year (our illustration).

One caution: both Frigidaire sheets we checked list an IEF of 1.8 and say "ENERGY STAR Certified Yes." That is below the current Version 6.0 threshold for their size, which suggests those certifications predate Version 6.0. Check the current ENERGY STAR list before you buy if efficiency matters to you.

## How to Cut the Cost of Running a Dehumidifier

1. **Set the humidistat no lower than needed.** ENERGY STAR says the optimum indoor relative humidity is "generally considered to be between 30% and 50%," and 30% to 40% in cold climates during the heating season to prevent window condensation. Every extra point lower means more run time.
2. **Close the room.** A dehumidifier in an open basement stairwell dries the whole house. Shut doors and windows in the space you are drying.
3. **Fix the source.** Gutters, downspouts and dryer vents that dump moisture near the foundation make the unit run longer.
4. **Size it to the space.** An undersized unit runs nonstop; check capacity under the post-2019 rating.
5. **Turn it off when the air is dry.** In heated winter air, many homes do not need a dehumidifier at all. In winter, our [electric fireplace cost guide](/posts/electric-fireplace-cost-to-run/) and [EnergyGuide label explainer](/posts/energyguide-label-explained/) cover other appliance costs.

## Does Solar Cover a Dehumidifier?

A dehumidifier's load is usually highest in humid summer months, when rooftop solar output is also high, so daytime running lines up reasonably well with solar production. At 999 kWh a year, a dehumidifier is a modest share of what a typical solar system produces, but on a bill where it runs all summer it is worth counting when you size a system.
