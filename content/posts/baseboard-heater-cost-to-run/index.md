**An electric baseboard heater costs its wattage times your electric rate for every hour its element is on. A common 4-foot, 1,000-watt heater uses 1 kWh an hour, about 18 cents at the U.S. average residential price of 18.16 cents per kWh for August 2026. A 1,500-watt heater costs about 27 cents an hour.** Your monthly bill depends on how many hours the thermostat actually keeps the element on, and on what you pay per kWh.

About 42% of U.S. households used electricity as their main heating fuel in 2024, according to the Energy Information Administration (EIA), and baseboards are a common electric option. This guide shows the math by heater size, room and whole house, and when a heat pump or zoning saves money. For a portable heater, see our [space heater running-cost guide](/posts/space-heater-cost-to-run/); to find your own rate, see [electricity rates by state](/rates-by-state/).

## The Formula

**Cost per hour = (watts ÷ 1,000) × your price per kWh**

Your bill charges for kilowatt-hours (kWh). A 1,000-watt heater running for one hour uses 1 kWh. Our [kW vs kWh explainer](/posts/kw-vs-kwh/) covers the difference.

The wattage is on the heater's label. Baseboard wattage generally scales with length. Cadet's F Series catalog, for example, lists 240-volt models at 500 watts (30 inches), 750 watts (36 inches), 1,000 watts (48 inches), 1,250 watts (60 inches), 1,500 watts (72 inches) and 2,000 watts (96 inches).

**Voltage does not change the cost.** A 1,000-watt heater uses 1 kWh an hour whether it is a 120-volt or 240-volt model. One exception: a 240-volt heater connected to 208-volt power, used in some apartment and commercial buildings, puts out less. Cadet lists its 4-foot 4F1000 at 1,000 watts on 240 volts and 750 watts on 208 volts. It uses less electricity per hour, but also delivers less heat, so it may run longer.

## Cost Per Hour by Heater Size

Assumptions: U.S. average residential price of 18.16 cents per kWh for August 2026 (EIA, released Oct. 23, 2026); element on at full rated output.

| Heater (240 V, typical length) | kWh per hour | Cost per hour at 18.16¢ |
|---|---|---|
| 500 W (about 2.5 ft) | 0.5 | $0.09 |
| 750 W (3 ft) | 0.75 | $0.14 |
| 1,000 W (4 ft) | 1.0 | $0.18 |
| 1,500 W (6 ft) | 1.5 | $0.27 |
| 2,000 W (8 ft) | 2.0 | $0.36 |

These are full-output figures. A baseboard heater does not use its full wattage every hour it is "on"; the thermostat cycles the element off once the room reaches the set temperature.

## What One Room Costs a Month

Take a 12-by-12-foot bedroom (144 square feet). A common rule of thumb from heater retailer Homelectrical is about 10 watts per square foot in a well-insulated room with 8-foot ceilings, which gives about 1,440 watts, so a 1,500-watt, 6-foot heater. The same guide suggests 12.5 watts per square foot for older homes and adding 25% for a 10-foot ceiling.

How long the element actually runs is the big unknown: it depends on the weather, your insulation and the thermostat setting. So here are three cases, on a 30-day month at 18.16 cents per kWh:

| Element on (hours a day) | kWh a month | Cost a month |
|---|---|---|
| 4 hours | 180 | $32.69 |
| 8 hours | 360 | $65.38 |
| 12 hours | 540 | $98.06 |

The math for 8 hours: 1.5 kW × 8 hours × 30 days = 360 kWh, and 360 × $0.1816 = $65.38.

The same room costs very different amounts by state. At 360 kWh a month and EIA's August 2026 state averages for residential customers:

| State (example) | Price per kWh (Aug. 2026) | 360 kWh a month |
|---|---|---|
| Washington | 14.63¢ | $52.67 |
| Minnesota | 17.20¢ | $61.92 |
| U.S. average | 18.16¢ | $65.38 |
| Maine | 32.11¢ | $115.60 |

State averages blend every utility in the state and include fixed charges spread over kWh, so your own bill's price per kWh is the better number to use. Winter rates can also differ from August rates.

## What a Whole House Costs

Baseboard costs add up when every room has a heater. Using the same 10-watts-per-square-foot rule, a 1,200-square-foot home would have about 12,000 watts of baseboard heat. With every heater at full output, that is 12 kWh an hour, about $2.18 an hour at 18.16 cents.

If those heaters together averaged the equivalent of 6 hours a day at full output over a 30-day month, an assumption for illustration, not a measurement, the house would use 2,160 kWh for heating, about $392. Your real figure could be far lower in a mild month or higher in a cold snap.

For a national benchmark, EIA's Winter Fuels Outlook forecasts that homes heating mainly with electricity will spend an average of $1,196 on electricity from November 2026 through March 2027, up 4% from last winter. EIA notes that the main heating fuel is also used for other purposes, so that figure covers more than heat. See our [winter heating costs outlook](/posts/winter-heating-costs-2026-27/) for the breakdown.

## How to Lower Baseboard Heating Costs

- **Heat the rooms you use.** Baseboards already give each room its own thermostat. The Department of Energy (DOE) says zone heating "can produce energy savings of more than 20% compared to heating the whole area of your house."
- **Upgrade the thermostat.** DOE says the built-in line-voltage thermostats on baseboards "often don't sense the room temperature accurately" and recommends a remote line-voltage or low-voltage thermostat on an interior wall. A programmable model also lets you lower the temperature at night or while you are out.
- **Keep the heater clear.** Baseboards work by convection: DOE explains that warmed air rises into the room and cooler air is drawn into the bottom. Furniture or long curtains in front of a heater block that flow.
- **Consider a heat pump.** DOE says electric resistance heating, which includes baseboards, is "100% energy efficient in the sense that all the incoming electric energy is converted to heat," but heat pumps "easily cut electricity use by 50% when compared with electric resistance heating." At that rate, the 360 kWh room in our example would need about 180 kWh. Ductless mini-split heat pumps are a common replacement in baseboard-heated homes. Our [heat pump buying guide](/posts/how-to-choose-a-heat-pump/) covers sizing and efficiency ratings.

## Does Solar Offset Baseboard Heat?

Only partly. Baseboard heat runs hardest on winter nights and cold mornings, when solar panels produce little or nothing, and panels produce less in winter overall (see [how solar output changes in winter](/posts/solar-panels-in-winter/)). Under net metering that credits exports at the full retail rate, summer surplus can offset winter heating kWh where credits roll over month to month. Where exports earn less than retail, more of baseboard heat is bought from the grid at the full price. Cutting the heating load first, with a heat pump, better thermostats and air sealing, usually lets a smaller solar system cover more of the bill.
