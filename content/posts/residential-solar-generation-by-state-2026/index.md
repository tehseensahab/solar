The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) estimates that small-scale solar at homes produced 42,910 thousand megawatt-hours (MWh) from January through July 2026. One thousand MWh is one million kilowatt-hours (kWh), so that is about 42.9 billion kWh. The same months of 2025 produced 38,428 thousand MWh, so output grew 11.7%.

These figures come from EIA's Electric Power Monthly, Table 1.17.B, which was released on September 24, 2026 and covers data through July 2026. EIA marks the values preliminary. Below is the state-by-state picture and what it can and cannot tell you.

## What the Table Measures

EIA reports solar generation by state and by sector. We use one column: **residential sector, estimated small-scale generation**. In plain terms, that is EIA's estimate of the electricity produced by small solar systems at homes. Two points matter for reading it.

- **It is an estimate.** EIA says its small-scale solar estimates are based on data from utility survey forms (Forms EIA-861M and EIA-861) and on estimation methods described in its technical notes. They are not readings from each home's meter.
- **It is generation, not installations.** The figure is the electricity produced during January through July. It is not the number of systems installed or the capacity added.

Commercial and industrial small-scale solar are separate columns in the same table and are not included in our numbers. Utility-scale solar plants are also excluded.

All figures are thousand MWh, January through July. Growth and share are our calculations from EIA's rounded figures.

**Formulas:** change = (2026 ÷ 2025) − 1. Share = state ÷ 42,910. The 50 states and the District of Columbia sum to 42,912 because of EIA's rounding.

Three things stand out:

- **Concentration.** California alone produced 36.3% of the total. The top four states (California, Florida, Texas, Arizona) produced 57.9%, and the top ten produced 76.5%.
- **California's growth is below the national rate.** Its output rose 9.4%, against 11.7% for the U.S. Without California, the other 49 states and D.C. grew 13.0% (27,315 ÷ 24,173 − 1). California still supplied 1,340 of the 4,482 thousand MWh added nationally, or 29.9%.
- **The big sun-belt states grew more slowly than much of the Northeast.** Texas (+6.2%) and Arizona (+6.9%) trail New Jersey (+17.9%), Massachusetts (+19.1%) and Connecticut (+18.8%).

## Fastest and Slowest Growth

We limited this comparison to the 22 states with at least 300 thousand MWh in January through July 2026. Smaller bases can swing wildly on small changes: West Virginia's +35.3% is 12 thousand MWh, and Vermont's +35.5% is 33 thousand MWh.

| Fastest growth (≥300 thousand MWh) | Change | Slowest growth (≥300 thousand MWh) | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pennsylvania | +27.2% | South Carolina | +4.3% |
| Illinois | +26.0% | New Mexico | +5.4% |
| Massachusetts | +19.1% | Texas | +6.2% |
| Connecticut | +18.8% | Utah | +6.4% |
| New Jersey | +17.9% | Arizona | +6.9% |

Growth in output does not tell you why it grew. Pennsylvania and Illinois are well above the national rate, but EIA's table does not say whether that comes from newly installed systems, systems added late in 2025 that ran for all seven months of 2026, or weather. In Illinois, systems connected on or after January 1, 2025 are on net billing rather than full retail net metering, as we explain in our [Illinois guide](/posts/illinois-electricity-rate-changes-2026/). A rule change like that can affect when people install, but this data cannot confirm it did.

## Is Growth Linked to Rising Electricity Prices?

Our [July 2026 price comparison](/posts/electricity-prices-by-state-july-2026/) reported that residential electricity prices were up more than 10% from a year earlier in Maryland, Ohio, Illinois, Delaware, Pennsylvania and Virginia. Home solar output grew faster than the 11.7% national rate in five of those six: Maryland (+17.5%), Ohio (+22.5%), Illinois (+26.0%), Pennsylvania (+27.2%) and Virginia (+12.9%). Delaware (+7.8%) did not.

That looks like a pattern, but it is not a conclusion. Connecticut and New Jersey saw prices fall in July 2026 (−12.5% and −0.5%) and still grew faster than average (+18.8% and +17.9%). Output also depends on how many systems were installed in earlier years. Two data points per state cannot separate price effects from everything else, so we are not claiming that higher prices caused more solar.

## What These Numbers Do Not Show

- **They do not show 2026 installations.** A state with fast growth may have added many systems in 2025 that ran for all seven months of 2026, while a system installed in July adds little.
- **They are not adjusted for state size.** California has far more homes than Vermont. We did not calculate per-household or per-person figures, because we did not pair this table with household counts.
- **Weather matters.** The comparison uses the same seven months in both years, which keeps the period like-for-like. It does not remove differences in cloud cover or temperature between the two years.
- **They do not show savings.** How much a home saves from its output depends on its utility's retail rate and how exported power is credited. See our guides to [net metering by state](/posts/net-metering-state-guide/) and [export credit rates by utility](/posts/solar-export-credit-rates-by-utility-2026/).
- **They are preliminary.** EIA says the values are preliminary, and the next Electric Power Monthly release is scheduled for October 23, 2026. Estimates can be revised.

## What This Means for Homeowners

Output growth of 11.7% tells you the installed base of home solar is still producing more each year. It does not tell you whether solar is a good decision for your home. For that, use your utility's current rate and credit rules, a written quote, and a payback calculation that does not assume the federal credit. Our [guide to comparing solar quotes](/posts/how-to-compare-solar-quotes/) and our [payback method without the federal tax credit](/posts/solar-payback-period-without-federal-tax-credit/) show how. Our [rates by state](/rates-by-state/) pages cover each state's current utility rates.

## Limitations

We use one EIA table and have not checked it against another dataset such as installed capacity. EIA's table did not report a percentage change for the residential column, so all percentages here are our own calculations from rounded figures. We did not confirm how EIA defines "small-scale" in this table beyond the notes quoted above. The data covers January through July 2026 only.
