Texas now has a registration requirement for the people and companies that sell or lease solar panels for homes. It took effect on September 1, 2026 under Senate Bill 1036, passed by the 89th Texas Legislature in 2025 and administered by the Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation (TDLR). Some consumer protections in the same law have applied since September 1, 2025, and two of TDLR's rules are paused until November 1, 2026. This guide sorts out which is which, using the statute, TDLR's own pages and the adopted rules.

**Scope:** Texas only, and only for residential solar sold or leased under the law (details below). It does not cover the federal tax credit or your utility's export credits, though we point to our guides on both.

## Who is covered, and who is not

TDLR says the law covers sales and leases of residential solar energy systems intended primarily for family, personal or household use, including solar panels and solar shingles. It says covered agreements include power purchase agreements (PPAs), where the consumer does not buy or lease the panels but lets them be installed on the property and agrees to buy the electricity they generate.

The Act does not apply to:

- systems on multifamily buildings with four or more dwelling units or stories;
- systems on nonresidential property;
- systems intended only for temporary or emergency use, to power a single appliance, or certain small systems generating less than one kilowatt of peak output in total.

**Electrical contractors** are exempt from the retailer and salesperson registration and the related insurance requirements, but TDLR says they must still follow most other provisions, including the contract and cancellation rules.

## What a Texas solar contract must contain

For a sale or lease that involves installing a system at a person's residence, TDLR says the agreement must:

1. Provide that a **licensed electrical contractor** will perform the installation.
2. **Conspicuously state the contractor's name and license number.** A list of contractors from which one must be chosen also satisfies this.
3. Provide that the retailer or contractor will obtain any government **permits** required, and, where applicable, the **utility's approval of the interconnection** (the connection to the grid). For customers of an electric cooperative or a municipally owned utility, the contract must provide for that cooperative's or utility's approval.
4. If a **lender** is affiliated with or referred by the retailer, require the lender to **cancel the loan** if you cancel the agreement.

## The five-business-day right to cancel

A buyer or lessee can cancel without penalty or further obligation by written notice on or before the fifth business day after the agreement was signed. The seller must put in the contract the last calendar date of the cancellation period and a mailing or email address for notices. If the address is missing, you can cancel by any reasonable written method.

Practical points that follow from that rule:

- Use the date printed in your contract as your deadline, and send notice in writing in a way you can prove, with a copy kept.
- A contract that omits the cancellation date or address does not meet the law's requirements. Keep it and contact TDLR.
- If a lender was arranged through the seller, the contract should say the loan is cancelled when the solar agreement is.

This applies to agreements signed on or after September 1, 2025. TDLR cannot take complaints about retailers for earlier contracts.

## Registration: how to check a seller

Beginning September 1, 2026, retailers and salespersons must be registered with TDLR to sell or lease residential solar. The statute says a person may not do residential solar retail for pay unless acting for a registered retailer and registered as a salesperson. TDLR's program page links a license search and a "Verify a License" tool.

A quick check before you sign:

1. Search TDLR for the **company** (solar retailer registration) and the **individual** who visited you (solar salesperson registration), unless they work for a licensed electrical contractor, which is exempt from registering.
2. Look up the **electrical contractor** named in the contract on TDLR's license search.
3. Read the contract for the four required items above and the cancellation date.
4. Keep copies of everything, including any disclosure form.

We did not verify registration fees or insurance minimums. TDLR's program page does not list them, and the rule text we read did not state amounts, though one trade outlet has reported figures.

## Prohibited acts

The statute's list of prohibited acts, which TDLR says became enforceable on September 1, 2026, includes:

- making a false, misleading or deceptive statement in residential solar retail, when done intentionally, knowingly or recklessly;
- falsely stating or implying an affiliation with a public utility or government agency;
- failing to provide required disclosure statements or educational materials;
- selling at a residence that has posted no-soliciting signage, unless an occupant directs otherwise;
- allowing installation by anyone who is not an electrical contractor.

The law sets civil penalty caps in court actions: up to $2,500 per violation or $50,000 in total for similar violations, rising to $10,000 and $100,000 if a court finds the victim was over 65. These are statutory ceilings, not typical outcomes. TDLR can also issue warning letters, cease and desist orders, and order contract cancellation and refunds up to the amount the consumer paid.

If a seller tells you something that sounds too good to be true, test it. For example, the IRS says the federal Residential Clean Energy Credit "will not be allowed for any expenditures made after December 31, 2025," and treats an expenditure as made when the original installation is completed. A homeowner-owned system finished in 2026 does not qualify, so a claim that you can still get that 30% credit on a purchase installed in 2026 contradicts IRS guidance. See [how to calculate payback without the federal credit](/posts/solar-payback-period-without-federal-tax-credit/) and [how to compare solar quotes](/posts/how-to-compare-solar-quotes/).

## What is paused until November 1, 2026

On August 21, 2026 TDLR announced it was "temporarily suspending enforcement" of two administrative rules until November 1, 2026: §71.41 (requirements of contract and disclosures) and §71.43 (educational materials). It said the aim is to give retailers extra time to adjust. A summary of the adopted rules says §71.43 requires a salesperson to give the consumer TDLR's educational brochure at least 24 hours before the contract is signed. That requirement is within the paused rule, so do not assume a seller must hand you the brochure before November 1, 2026. The paused rules are separate from the cancellation right, the required contract terms in the statute, and registration, which TDLR's Aug. 21 notice does not mention changing.

## If something goes wrong

- **Contract signed on or after September 1, 2025:** file a complaint with TDLR through its website, or call (512) 463-6599 or (800) 803-9202.
- **Contract signed before September 1, 2025:** TDLR says to contact the Texas Attorney General's Office of Consumer Protection.
- **Installation or repair work by an electrical contractor:** TDLR says it accepts complaints about electrical contractors for solar work performed at any time.

TDLR does not guarantee a timeline for investigations. Our guide to [comparing solar quotes](/posts/how-to-compare-solar-quotes/) covers what to ask before you sign anything.

This is general information about a state law, not legal advice.
