At the end of 2025, U.S. electric utilities reported 5,513,622 residential solar systems enrolled in net metering across the 50 states and the District of Columbia. That is 8.6% more than a year earlier, according to final data the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) released on October 7, 2026.

**Net metering** is a billing arrangement in which a customer with an on-site generator, usually solar panels, is credited for power sent to the grid. EIA's form defines it as an arrangement that lets a customer sell excess power back to the grid, "typically but not necessarily at a rate equivalent to the retail price of electricity." Net-metering rules differ widely by state and utility. See our [net metering state guide](/posts/net-metering-state-guide/).

## The Short Version

| Measure (residential, photovoltaic, net metered; 50 states + D.C.) | End of 2024 | End of 2025 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Systems enrolled | 5,077,543 | 5,513,622 | +436,079 (+8.6%) |
| Capacity | 34,226 MW | 37,830 MW | +3,603 MW (+10.5%) |

A megawatt (MW) is 1,000 kilowatts (kW). 37,830 MW is about 37.8 gigawatts.

**Net change, not installations.** EIA asks utilities to report the number of net-metering systems "actively enrolled" as of December 31. The 436,079 increase is the difference between two year-end counts. It does not show how many systems were installed in 2025, because systems that left the program or were counted differently would lower the net figure.

California alone has 40.7% of the systems (2,241,381 ÷ 5,513,622) and 36.4% of the capacity (13,787 ÷ 37,830 MW). The five states with the most systems, California, Arizona, Florida, New York and New Jersey, hold 61.0% of the national count. Thirteen states have more than 100,000 systems.

Because California is so large, it also drives the national change: it accounts for 150,398 of the 436,079 net additions, or 34%. Excluding California, the number of systems rose 9.6%, a faster rate than California's 7.2%.

## Systems Relative to Residential Customers

A raw count favors big states. To compare, we divided each state's systems by its number of residential customers, using the 2025 customer counts from EIA's [average residential bill table](/posts/average-home-electricity-use-by-state-2025/).

Only four states reach 10 or more systems per 100 customers: Hawaii, California, Nevada and Arizona. The U.S. figure is 3.8 (5,513,622 systems ÷ 144,800,940 residential customers).

**Read this ratio carefully.** The numerator is a count of net-metered systems or customers, and the denominator is all residential customers, including renters, apartment dwellers and anyone who cannot install panels. It is not the share of homes that have solar.

## Where Growth Was Fastest

Among the 20 states that already had at least 50,000 systems at the end of 2024, the fastest growth in the number of systems in 2025 was in:

- Oregon, +17.0%
- Illinois, +15.8%
- Pennsylvania, +15.4%
- Connecticut, +15.1%
- Washington, +11.9%

The slowest were Texas (+3.4%), Arizona (+5.3%), New Mexico (+5.4%) and Hawaii (+5.9%). Systems rose in all 51 areas. Capacity rose in 50 of 51; South Dakota's reported capacity fell slightly, from 3.633 MW to 3.572 MW, while its system count rose from 469 to 570.

Percentage growth is easier to achieve from a small base, so compare it with the absolute change. California added 150,398 systems, Florida 29,637, New York 24,849, Massachusetts 20,382 and New Jersey 19,573.

## What This Does Not Tell You

- **It is not all residential solar.** EIA collects non-net-metered generators on a separate schedule, and third-party-owned systems are reported on a separate tab. We did not add those figures, to avoid double counting.
- **Virtual net metering is separate.** EIA reports virtual net metering (for example, a customer receiving credit from a shared off-site system) in separate columns, which these totals exclude.
- **Capacity units are mixed.** EIA's instructions let utilities report photovoltaic capacity in either AC (alternating current) or DC (direct current) megawatts. State capacity figures are not strictly comparable.
- **Reporting varies.** Some states show very few systems: Alabama (87), North Dakota (89), Tennessee (97) and South Dakota (570). A low count here does not prove there is little solar in those states, because it reflects only what utilities report as net metering.
- **The data cannot say why numbers moved.** The federal Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) ended for expenditures made after December 31, 2025, according to the IRS, so 2025 was the last year a homeowner could complete a purchased system and claim it. EIA's counts do not show whether that deadline changed installation timing, and we draw no causal link.

## What It Means for Homeowners Considering Solar

The map of where solar is common is not a map of where it pays. Payback depends on your use, your utility's rates and what it credits for exported power. Utilities within one state can differ. For example, see how [export credits compare by utility](/posts/solar-export-credit-rates-by-utility-2026/), run the [payback calculator](/solar-payback-calculator/), and check your state's [rate changes](/rates-by-state/).

These figures end on December 31, 2025. EIA's data page lists its next release as August 2027 (early release data), which will be the first to cover 2026.

## Limitations

- We summed EIA's utility-level rows to produce state totals for 2024, because the 2024 file has no state-level tab. The same method reproduces EIA's own 2025 state tab exactly for every state. State totals include EIA's state-level adjustment rows.
- The 2024 comparison uses EIA's 2024 file as published October 2025. EIA can revise earlier years, and we did not check for revisions.
- The ratio of systems to customers is our calculation and mixes two EIA concepts: net-metered systems or customers, and residential customer accounts.
- EIA's 2025 download includes form instructions labeled 2024. We used their definitions of net metering and capacity reporting only.
