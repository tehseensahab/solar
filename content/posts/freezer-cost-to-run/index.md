**A U.S. home with a separate freezer used an average of 570 kWh of electricity a year on it in 2020, according to the Energy Information Administration (EIA). At the U.S. average residential price of 18.16 cents per kWh for August 2026, that is about $103.51 a year, $8.63 a month or 28 cents a day.** A new chest freezer can cost half that: three models we checked are rated at 297 to 331 kWh a year, about $54 to $60.

A freezer runs all day, every day, so its cost shows up on every bill. This guide shows the math per day, month and year, compares chest and upright models from their EnergyGuide labels, and shows how the cost changes by state. For the fridge in your kitchen, see our [refrigerator cost to run](/posts/refrigerator-cost-to-run/) guide. To use your own price, look it up on our [electricity rates by state](/rates/) page.

## The Formula

**Cost = kWh used × your price per kWh**

Your price per kWh is on your bill. If your bill splits supply and delivery charges, add the per-kWh parts together and leave out the fixed monthly customer charge. Our [kW vs kWh explainer](/posts/kw-vs-kwh/) covers the units.

## How Much Does It Cost to Run a Freezer?

About $8.63 a month for the average U.S. home with a separate freezer, and roughly $4.50 to $8 a month for the new models we checked. All costs below use 18.16 cents per kWh.

| Freezer | kWh a year | Per day | Per month | Per year |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| U.S. average, homes with a separate freezer (EIA, 2020, per household) | 570 | $0.28 | $8.63 | $103.51 |
| Amana 14.8 cu ft chest, manual defrost (AZC31T15D) | 297 | $0.15 | $4.49 | $53.94 |
| Amana 16.0 cu ft chest, manual defrost (AZC5216LW) | 313 | $0.16 | $4.74 | $56.84 |
| White-Westinghouse 17.5 cu ft chest, manual defrost (CR4M81CFMM) | 331 | $0.17 | $5.01 | $60.11 |
| Frigidaire 17.3 cu ft upright, automatic defrost (FUF17DL) | 441 | $0.22 | $6.67 | $80.09 |
| Whirlpool 19.8 cu ft upright, automatic defrost (WZF79R20D) | 525 | $0.26 | $7.95 | $95.34 |

Assumptions: EIA Residential Energy Consumption Survey (RECS) 2020, Table CE5.3b, average electricity use per household that has a separate freezer; model ratings from each model's yellow EnergyGuide label; U.S. average residential price of 18.16 cents per kWh for August 2026 (EIA, released October 23, 2026; preliminary). Monthly figures are the yearly figure divided by 12.

**Why the average is higher than new models.** EIA's 570 kWh is a 2020 average across freezers of every age and size in U.S. homes. The labels are lab ratings for new models under a federal test. Your freezer's real use depends on its age, where it sits (a hot garage makes it work harder), how full it is and how often it is opened.

## Chest vs. Upright Freezer: Which Costs Less to Run?

A chest freezer usually costs less. The Department of Energy says "Chest freezers are more efficient than upright models because they typically have more insulation" and "cold air does not sink out of them when they are opened."

The labels we checked agree:

- **Similar size, different type.** The 17.5-cubic-foot chest model is rated at 331 kWh a year; the 17.3-cubic-foot upright at 441 kWh. That 110 kWh gap is about $20 a year at 18.16 cents, or about $200 over 10 years at today's price.
- **Defrost type differs too.** All three chest models are manual defrost; both uprights are automatic (frost-free). Labels do not split out how much of the gap comes from the defrost system, so compare the kWh line on the specific models you are considering.

An upright is easier to organize and takes less floor space. If you open it often to find things, that convenience may be worth the extra $20 or more a year in our comparison. If it holds bulk food you rarely touch, a chest model is the cheaper choice.

## How to Read a Freezer's EnergyGuide Label

The large number in the middle is estimated yearly cost, but it is based on an old electricity price. The Frigidaire and Amana 16-cubic-foot labels use "a national average electricity cost of 14 cents per kWh"; the Amana 14.8-cubic-foot and White-Westinghouse labels use 12 cents. At today's 18.16-cent average, the label cost understates what you will pay.

Use the **kWh per year** figure instead and multiply it by your own price. For the Frigidaire upright: 441 kWh × $0.1816 = $80.09 a year, versus the $62 printed on its label at 14 cents.

The label also shows a "cost range of similar models." For the Frigidaire upright that is $58 to $89 at 14 cents, so models of that size and type vary by about $31 a year at the label's price.

## Is It Worth Replacing an Old Freezer?

It depends on how much your current one uses. Two rough comparisons at 18.16 cents:

- **Average freezer to new chest model:** 570 kWh minus 331 kWh is 239 kWh a year, about $43 a year.
- **Average freezer to new frost-free upright:** 570 kWh minus 441 kWh is 129 kWh a year, about $23 a year.

At those amounts, a new freezer takes years to pay for itself on energy alone unless your price is high or your old unit uses far more than average. To find out, plug the freezer into a plug-in electricity monitor for a week, multiply the kWh by 52, and compare that with the label of the model you are considering. If the old freezer is mostly empty, unplugging it saves the full cost.

## How to Cut Your Freezer's Running Cost

1. **Set it between −5°F and 0°F.** That is the Department of Energy's range; colder wastes energy, and DOE warns that going above 0°F shortens how long food keeps.
2. **Keep it out of heat.** A freezer in a hot garage or next to an oven has to remove more heat.
3. **Buy the size you will fill.** DOE says oversized freezers "waste energy because of unused capacity."
4. **Choose chest over upright** if you rarely need to get at the food.
5. **Defrost manual models** when frost builds up, as the manufacturer's manual directs.

## Freezers, Power Outages and Home Batteries

FoodSafety.gov says a full freezer holds its temperature "about 48 hours, or 24 hours if it is half-full, if you don't open the door." A freezer also needs little backup energy: the Frigidaire upright's 441 kWh a year averages about 1.2 kWh a day. If you are sizing a battery for outages, that daily figure is a better guide than the compressor's running watts; our [battery sizing guide](/posts/battery-sizing-guide/) shows how to add up loads.

**Who this is for:** households estimating what a garage or basement freezer adds to their bill, or choosing between models. If your freezer is part of a refrigerator, use our refrigerator guide instead.

## Limitations

EIA's freezer figure is from its 2020 survey and is an average across all ages and sizes; newer survey results are not out yet. EnergyGuide ratings are lab results for one model each under a federal test, and real use depends on temperature setting, room temperature, how full the freezer is and how often it is opened. State prices are monthly averages, may not include sales taxes, and change month to month.
