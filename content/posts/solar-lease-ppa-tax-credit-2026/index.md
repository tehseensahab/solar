If you buy solar panels for your home in 2026, there is no federal tax credit to subtract from the price. If you lease them or buy their output under a power purchase agreement (PPA), you never claimed a credit in the first place. What changed is a different credit that the company owning the system may be able to claim, and which has a deadline. This guide explains both credits, the dates, and what you can and cannot conclude from them when a salesperson says "the tax credit is built into your payment."

## The Short Answer

| | Buying the system | Leasing or PPA |
|---|---|---|
| Who owns the equipment | You | The company |
| Credit you can claim | None. The Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) ended for property placed in service after December 31, 2025 | None. The Federal Trade Commission says credits go to the system's owner |
| Credit the owner may claim | Not applicable | The Clean Electricity Investment Credit (Section 48E), if the facility qualifies and meets the deadlines below |
| Is any credit passed to you? | Not applicable | Not required by law. It depends on the company's pricing and contract |

These are federal rules for residential solar-electric systems in the United States. State and utility incentives are separate.

## What Ended: Section 25D

The Residential Clean Energy Credit, Section 25D of the Internal Revenue Code, paid homeowners 30% of the cost of qualifying equipment. The IRS states the credit "is not available for any property placed in service after December 31, 2025." The statute words it slightly differently: the credit "shall not apply with respect to any expenditures made after December 31, 2025." The IRS says an expenditure is treated as made when the original installation is completed, so a system finished in 2026 does not qualify, even if you paid earlier.

Section 25D has always been a credit for the person who spends the money. It covers property used as a residence "by the taxpayer." A homeowner leasing a system has not bought it, so there was never a 25D credit to claim. Our [solar payback guide](/posts/solar-payback-period-without-federal-tax-credit/) shows the math for buyers without it.

## What Remains: Section 48E for the Owner

Section 48E, the Clean Electricity Investment Credit, is a business credit. It is claimed by the taxpayer that places a qualified facility in service, such as a company that owns solar panels on a customer's roof.

Three points from the statute matter here.

- **Leased solar-electric property is not excluded.** Section 48E(i) denies the credit for leased property described in Section 25D(d)(1) and (d)(4). Those are solar water heating and small wind systems. Solar-electric property is paragraph (d)(2), which is not on the list.
- **The rate depends on size.** The statute sets a 6% base rate and a 30% rate for a facility with a maximum net output under 1 megawatt (measured in alternating current), among other routes. A home system is a few kilowatts, far below 1 megawatt. This tells you the rate the law sets for a facility of that size. It does not tell you what any company's credit was worth on a specific home.
- **There is an end date for solar.** Section 48E does not apply to solar-electric property placed in service after December 31, 2027. The exception is a facility whose construction began on or before July 4, 2026.

That last date has now passed. Today is October 7, 2026. So for a new lease or PPA contract signed now, the question is which of two paths a given system is on:

1. **Construction began on or before July 4, 2026.** IRS Notice 2025-42 says that if a facility is placed in service by the end of the fourth calendar year after the year construction began, it is treated as meeting the continuity requirement. For construction starting in 2026, that is December 31, 2030.
2. **Construction had not begun.** The system must be placed in service by December 31, 2027, about 15 months from today, to be eligible.

## How Construction Start Is Proven

Notice 2025-42 applies to facilities whose construction did not begin before September 2, 2025. For solar, it makes the "Physical Work Test," which looks at whether physical work of a significant nature has started, the main way to show construction began before July 5, 2026. One exception: a "low output" solar facility, with maximum net output of 1.5 megawatts or less (alternating current), may alternatively use the Five Percent Safe Harbor, which is based on costs paid or incurred.

We could not find public information on how residential lease and PPA companies apply these tests to their pipelines of home systems. Whether the system on your roof is eligible is a question for the company and its tax advisers, not something you can read off a public list.

## What This Does Not Tell You

A credit available to the owner is not a discount to you. Three limits apply.

- **No pass-through rule.** Nothing we found requires a company to lower your payment because it claims a credit. Pricing is set by contract.
- **No guaranteed savings.** The Department of Energy says that in general a purchased system can be installed at a lower total cost than one using a loan, lease or PPA. A lease can still make sense if you do not want to put cash down. Compare it on the numbers.
- **Terms run long.** The FTC says these contracts can last 20 years, and that monthly lease payments can increase over time. Its guide also says the contract may affect your options when you sell your house.

Batteries are different. The Section 48E termination for wind and solar says it does not apply to energy storage technology at an applicable facility. We did not research whether any company offers leased batteries or how it prices them.

## What to Ask a Lease or PPA Salesperson

1. **What is the starting payment or price per kWh, and the yearly increase?** Calculate the year-20 payment: starting payment × (1 + increase)^19.
2. **Does your pricing assume a federal credit? Which one, and has construction begun?** If the answer is vague, treat the savings claim as unverified.
3. **What happens if the credit is unavailable?** Ask whether the price can change after signing.
4. **What happens if I sell the house?** Ask about transfer terms and fees.
5. **What does the savings estimate assume for my rate and export credit?** Our guide to [comparing solar quotes](/posts/how-to-compare-solar-quotes/) covers these numbers, and our table of [export credit rates by utility](/posts/solar-export-credit-rates-by-utility-2026/) shows how much they vary.

This is general information, not tax or legal advice. Ask a qualified tax professional about your own situation.
