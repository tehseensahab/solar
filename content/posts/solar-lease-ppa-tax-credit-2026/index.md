A lease or power purchase agreement (PPA) customer does not get a federal tax credit, because the company that owns the system claims any credit, and that company's credit has a deadline. The Section 48E credit stops applying to solar facilities placed in service after December 31, 2027, but only those whose construction began after July 4, 2026. Facilities that began construction on or before July 4, 2026 have more time. That deadline for starting construction has now passed, so what matters for a new lease or PPA is whether the provider can document an earlier start for the system it would install on your roof.

This article explains what the statute and IRS guidance say, what remains unknown, and what to ask. It is general information, not tax or legal advice.

## Terms Used Here

- **Lease:** a company owns the system on your roof and you pay a monthly amount. **PPA (power purchase agreement):** the company owns the system and you pay for the electricity it produces at a price the company sets. The FTC notes contracts of this kind can last 20 years and payments may increase over time.
- **Section 25D:** the Residential Clean Energy Credit, a 30% credit for homeowners who buy a system.
- **Section 48E:** the Clean Electricity Investment Credit, a business credit for owners of qualifying facilities. A company that owns leased or PPA systems is the kind of taxpayer that would claim a business credit; we have not seen any provider's filings, so we cannot say which ones do.
- **Placed in service:** when a system is installed and ready to operate. **Beginning of construction:** a separate, earlier test of when the project started, defined in IRS guidance.

## Which Dates Matter

| Date | What happens | Applies to | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dec. 31, 2025 | Section 25D credit not available for property placed in service after this date | Homeowners who buy and own a system | IRS |
| July 4, 2026 | Last day to begin construction without being subject to the 48E termination | Business owners of solar facilities, including those who lease or sell power to homes | P.L. 119-21 §70513(g)(5); IRS Notice 2025-42 |
| Dec. 31, 2027 | 48E does not apply to solar facilities placed in service after this date, if construction began after July 4, 2026 | Same | P.L. 119-21 §70513(a) |

Note the difference between the two 2026–2027 dates. July 4, 2026 is about when the project began. December 31, 2027 is about when it is finished. A facility that began construction by July 4, 2026 is not subject to the December 31, 2027 cutoff, although IRS guidance sets a four-calendar-year safe harbor for finishing it (see below).

## What the Statute Says

The One, Big, Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21, signed July 4, 2025) added three rules relevant here.

1. **Termination.** New Section 48E(e)(4) says the credit "shall not apply to any qualified property placed in service by the taxpayer after December 31, 2027, which is part of an applicable facility," and an applicable facility includes one that uses solar energy to produce electricity. This applies to facilities whose construction begins after the date 12 months after enactment, that is, after July 4, 2026.
2. **Leasing bar, narrow.** New Section 48E(i) denies the credit for property described in paragraph (1) or (4) of Section 25D(d) if the owner rents or leases it to a third party. Under Section 25D(d), paragraph (1) is solar water heating property and paragraph (4) is small wind energy property. Paragraph (2), solar electric property (rooftop PV), is not listed. Some online guides describe the leasing bar as ending credits for leased rooftop solar; the statute's text names solar water heaters and small wind only.
3. **Foreign-entity restrictions.** Section 48E(b)(6) excludes a facility whose construction begins after December 31, 2025 if it includes "material assistance" from a prohibited foreign entity. Treasury's Notice 2026-15 gives interim safe harbors for measuring that. This applies to the business credit and is separate from the dates above; we did not assess how it affects any provider.

## What IRS Guidance Says About "Beginning of Construction"

Notice 2025-42, issued August 15, 2025, said a taxpayer can establish that construction began either through physical work of a significant nature or, only for "low output solar facilities," through the older Five Percent Safe Harbor (spending at least 5% of the cost). A low output solar facility is a solar facility with maximum net output of 1.5 megawatts or less in alternating current. A 10-kilowatt home system is 0.01 megawatts, far below that. The notice also says that a facility placed in service within four calendar years after the year construction began is treated as having satisfied the continuity requirement. For construction beginning in 2026, that is a placed-in-service date by the end of 2030.

For larger facilities, the notice removed the 5% option. **In June 2026 a court set aside that notice.** According to law-firm summaries, on June 6, 2026 the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia vacated Notice 2025-42 in *Oregon Environmental Council v. Internal Revenue Service*, finding it arbitrary and capricious. A September 4, 2026 Clifford Chance briefing reported that the government had not filed a notice of appeal as of that date, and that its appeal deadline had passed on August 5, 2026, subject to a possible extension motion. We could not access the court's opinion or docket, and we have not confirmed whether anything was filed after September 4. For home-sized systems the vacatur changes little, because the 5% option was already open to them.

## What Is Not Public

We found no Treasury or IRS guidance specific to residential lease or PPA fleets. We also found no public data showing which providers began construction on which systems before July 4, 2026, how they documented it, or how they treat the credit in pricing. So there is no basis to say that lease or PPA prices will rise or fall because of these dates, or that any particular provider's systems qualify.

## What to Do If You Are Offered a Lease or PPA

1. **Compare on price, not on the credit.** Look at the starting payment, the yearly escalator, the year-20 payment and the exit cost. Our guide to [comparing solar quotes](/posts/how-to-compare-solar-quotes/) has the checklist.
2. **Ask who owns the system and whether the owner expects to claim a federal credit for it.** The answer is the provider's, not ours; get it in writing if it affects the price.
3. **Ask when the provider's project for your system began construction, if the credit is part of the pricing.** A provider may decline to say. That is information too.
4. **Do not expect a tax benefit yourself.** The FTC says lease and PPA customers are not eligible for tax credits.
5. **If you are buying instead,** the 30% credit no longer applies to systems placed in service after 2025. See [solar payback without the federal tax credit](/posts/solar-payback-period-without-federal-tax-credit/).

## Limitations

This article describes the federal rules as of October 10, 2026. State incentives, utility rules and net metering are separate and vary; see our [net metering state guide](/posts/net-metering-state-guide/). Tax law and court proceedings can change. We have not read the Notice 2025-42 court opinion and rely on a law-firm briefing for its holding and the appeal status.
