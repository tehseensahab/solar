**If you buy a home solar system and it is installed in 2026, there is no federal tax credit for you.** The 30% Residential Clean Energy Credit, Section 25D of the Internal Revenue Code, ended for expenditures made after December 31, 2025. A different credit, Section 48E, still applies to solar facilities that a company owns, such as leased systems or power purchase agreements (PPAs), but it is claimed by that company, it has deadlines, and what you pay is set by your contract with the company, not by the tax code.

This guide separates what the statute and the IRS say from what we could not confirm. It covers the United States federal level only. State credits and utility rules are separate.

## What Ended: The Homeowner Credit (Section 25D)

Section 25D gave individual taxpayers a credit of 30% of the cost of qualified clean energy property for their home, including rooftop solar panels and battery storage of at least 3 kilowatt-hours (kWh) of capacity. The statute now says it "shall not apply with respect to any expenditures made after December 31, 2025," a change made by Public Law 119-21 (the law signed on July 4, 2025, commonly called the One Big Beautiful Bill Act). Before that change, the termination date in the statute was in 2034.

The IRS's frequently asked questions explain the timing rule: "An expenditure with respect to an item is treated as made when the original installation of the item is completed." So the date that matters is when installation was completed, not when you signed or paid. A system finished on or before December 31, 2025 falls on one side of the cutoff, and one finished on January 1, 2026 or later falls on the other.

One wording difference: the IRS's credit page, last reviewed July 4, 2026, says the credit "is not available for any property placed in service after December 31, 2025," while the statute and the IRS FAQs say "expenditures made." We rely on the statute and the FAQs. If your installation date is close to the cutoff, ask a tax professional.

## What Remains: The Business Credit (Section 48E)

Section 48E is the Clean Electricity Investment Credit. The IRS describes it as applying to a qualified facility and energy storage technology "placed in service after Dec. 31, 2024." It is a credit for the taxpayer that owns the property, typically a business. In a lease or PPA, that would be the company that owns the panels on your roof (our reading; confirm with your provider).

Two points are often garbled online, so here is what the statute text says:

1. **Leased property is restricted only for two technologies.** Section 48E says no credit is allowed for property described in paragraph (1) or (4) of Section 25D(d) if the owner rents or leases it to a third party. Those paragraphs define solar **water heating** property and **small wind** property. Solar **electric** property, which is what ordinary rooftop panels are, is defined in paragraph (2) and is not in that list.
2. **That is not a guarantee.** The absence of this specific restriction does not mean every lease or PPA earns a credit. Other conditions in Section 48E still apply, and we did not verify how each provider qualifies.

## The Deadlines for Solar Under Section 48E

For wind and solar facilities, the statute sets a cutoff in 26 U.S.C. 48E(e)(4)(A): the section "shall not apply to any qualified property placed in service by the taxpayer after December 31, 2027, which is part of an applicable facility." The statutory notes say the amendment applies to facilities "the construction of which begins after the date which is 12 months after July 4, 2025." That date is July 4, 2026.

| Situation | What the law and IRS guidance say | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Solar facility that began construction after July 4, 2026 | Must be placed in service by Dec. 31, 2027 to qualify under Section 48E | 26 U.S.C. 48E(e)(4) and statutory notes |
| Solar facility that began construction on or before July 4, 2026 | Not subject to that termination date. IRS Notice 2025-42 calls these facilities "not subject to the credit termination date." | IRS Notice 2025-42 |
| Energy storage (batteries) | Not part of the wind and solar termination rule | 26 U.S.C. 48E(e)(4) |
| Facility whose construction begins after Dec. 31, 2025 | Subject to the "prohibited foreign entity" material-assistance restrictions | 26 U.S.C. 48E |

Because today is October 6, 2026, the July 4, 2026 start date has passed. A new solar facility that did not already begin construction by then needs to be in service by December 31, 2027 to be eligible. Whether a particular provider's systems began construction in time is a fact about that provider, and we have not verified it for any company.

### How "beginning of construction" is determined

IRS Notice 2025-42, effective for facilities whose construction did not begin before September 2, 2025, sets the test. It eliminates the "Five Percent Safe Harbor" for most wind and solar facilities, which means taxpayers generally must show that "physical work of a significant nature" began. The exception is "low output solar facilities," defined as solar facilities with a maximum net output of not greater than 1.5 megawatts (alternating current), which may use either test to show construction began before July 5, 2026. The notice also provides a continuity safe harbor: a facility placed in service by the end of the fourth calendar year after construction began is treated as satisfying the continuity requirement.

This is guidance written for the companies claiming the credit. It tells a homeowner little about any single roof, but it explains why providers talk about "safe-harbored" equipment.

## What This Means If You Are Choosing How to Go Solar

- **Buying the system (cash or loan):** no federal credit for installations completed in 2026. Any savings must come from lower electricity bills, state or local incentives, or utility programs. Our [payback period guide](/posts/solar-payback-period-without-federal-tax-credit/) shows how to calculate it without the credit.
- **Leasing or a PPA:** you are not the taxpayer claiming a credit. You pay the company monthly, and the contract determines your price. Ask whether the price assumes a federal credit, and what happens to your price if the provider's eligibility changes. Compare the monthly payment and annual escalator against your expected savings.
- **Any option:** ask for the contract in writing, check your utility's rules for [net metering or net billing](/posts/net-metering-state-guide/), and see [what export credits pay](/posts/solar-export-credit-rates-by-utility-2026/).
