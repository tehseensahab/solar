**A typical electric hot tub uses about 1,963 kWh of electricity a year, based on Energy Information Administration (EIA) averages for 2020: 1,479 kWh for the heater and 484 kWh for the pump. At the U.S. average residential price of 18.16 cents per kWh for August 2026, that is about $356 a year, or about $30 a month.** Where you live matters a lot: the same usage costs about two-thirds more at California's average price ($584 a year) and more than 2.5 times as much in Hawaii ($926).

A hot tub is one of the larger electric loads a home can add, because it keeps hundreds of gallons of water hot around the clock. This guide shows the math, where the energy goes, how the cost changes by state and what lowers it. For the other big water-heating load in most homes, see our [electric water heater cost to run](/posts/electric-water-heater-cost-to-run/) guide.

## The Formula

**Cost = kWh used × your price per kWh**

Your price per kWh is on your bill. If your bill splits supply and delivery, add the per-kWh parts together and leave out the fixed monthly customer charge. Our [kW vs kWh explainer](/posts/kw-vs-kwh/) covers the units.

## How Much Does It Cost to Run a Hot Tub?

EIA's 2020 Residential Energy Consumption Survey (RECS) reports average electricity use per household that has each end use. Using those averages and EIA's August 2026 U.S. average price of 18.16 cents per kWh (released October 23, 2026):

| Part | kWh a year (EIA, 2020) | Cost a year | Cost a month |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hot tub heater | 1,479 | $268.59 | $22.38 |
| Hot tub pump | 484 | $87.89 | $7.32 |
| **Total** | **1,963** | **$356.48** | **$29.71** |

Per day, 1,963 kWh is about 5.4 kWh, or about 98 cents.

Two cautions. EIA averages heaters and pumps separately over the homes that have each one, so adding them assumes a tub with both an electric heater and a pump. And these are 2020 averages across all climates and tub sizes. A big tub in Minnesota will use more than a small one in Arizona.

### How much does a hot tub heater cost per hour?

A common heater size is 4 kW. For example, one 240-volt replacement element sold by Trevi is listed at "4.0KW /4000 watts" and 16.67 amps. Running for one hour, a 4 kW heater uses 4 kWh, which costs about 73 cents at 18.16 cents per kWh.

The heater does not run all day. It switches on to replace heat lost through the cover, shell and plumbing, and to reheat water after you use the tub. That is why the average heater uses 1,479 kWh a year rather than the 35,040 kWh a 4 kW element would use running nonstop.

### How long does it take to heat a hot tub?

Water needs about 1 Btu per pound to rise 1°F (the definition of a Btu), and a gallon weighs about 8.34 pounds. Raising 400 gallons by 10°F takes about 33,400 Btu, or about 9.8 kWh. With a 4 kW heater that is about 2.4 hours of heating and about $1.78 at 18.16 cents per kWh, before any heat lost along the way (our calculation). A cold fill that must rise 50°F or more takes several times as long.

## What California's Spa Rules Say About Standby Use

California regulates how much power a new portable electric spa may use in standby, that is, just holding its water temperature while no one is using it. For standard spas made on or after June 1, 2019, the California Energy Commission's limit is **3.75 × V^(2/3) + 40 watts**, where V is the fill volume in gallons, tested at a 37°F difference between water and air.

For a 400-gallon spa, that works out to about 244 watts on average. Over a year, that would be up to about 2,134 kWh, or about $387 at 18.16 cents per kWh (our calculation). That is a ceiling under test conditions, not a typical figure, and it does not include the energy to reheat water after use.

The rules also tie the result to the cover. The Energy Commission says a cover that "was not tested or tested but fails to meet the performance standard cannot be sold with the unit to the consumer." Spas sold in California must carry a label that is "conspicuously displayed to the consumer," so if you are shopping there, check that label.

## How to Lower What a Hot Tub Costs

- **Keep the cover on and in good shape.** California's rules only let a spa be sold with a cover that was tested with it and passed, which shows how much the cover matters for holding heat in. A cover that is waterlogged or no longer seals lets the heater run more.
- **Lower the temperature when you will not use it for a while.** The heater replaces heat lost to the air, and a smaller gap between water and air temperature means less loss.
- **Heat when power is cheapest.** If you are on a time-of-use plan, setting the spa to heat outside peak hours can cut the cost per kWh. Our [time-of-use rates and solar guide](/posts/time-of-use-rates-and-solar/) explains how those plans work.
- **Count it if you are sizing solar.** About 2,000 kWh a year is a meaningful addition to a home's usage. If you are getting solar quotes, make sure the installer's usage estimate includes the hot tub, especially if you added it after the last 12 months of bills.

## Who This Applies To

Electric hot tubs and portable spas. Tubs with gas heaters, in-ground spas heated by a pool heater, and wood-fired tubs use other fuels and are not covered by these figures. The cost figures use average prices; your bill may include fixed charges and taxes that do not change with usage.
