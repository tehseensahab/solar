**The yellow EnergyGuide label tells you how much energy one appliance model uses in a year and what that energy would cost at a fixed national average price.** For refrigerators, freezers, clothes washers, dishwashers and water heaters, new labels use 14 cents per kWh. The U.S. average residential price was 18.16 cents in August 2026, so the dollar figure on the label is usually low. The kWh figure is the one to trust: multiply it by your own price per kWh.

The label is still the best way to compare two models side by side, because every model in a category is tested and priced the same way. This guide explains each part of the label, the assumptions behind the cost, and how to turn it into your own number. For the units, see [kW vs kWh](/posts/kw-vs-kwh/).

## What Is the EnergyGuide Label?

The EnergyGuide label is a yellow tag required by the Federal Trade Commission's (FTC) Energy Labeling Rule, 16 CFR Part 305. The FTC's shopping guide describes the main cost figure as "only an estimate, based on typical use and a national average price for energy," and adds: "Your cost will depend on how you use the appliance and your local energy price."

The FTC lists these products among those that carry it: "boilers, ceiling fans, central air conditioners, clothes washers, dishwashers," "freezers, furnaces, heat pumps, pool heaters, refrigerators," "televisions, water heaters, room and portable air conditioners."

## How Do You Read an EnergyGuide Label?

From top to bottom, the parts that matter for a shopper are:

1. **Model and product type.** Labels compare models within a category, such as standard-size front-load washers or TVs of a similar screen size.
2. **Estimated yearly energy cost.** The large dollar figure. It is the yearly energy use multiplied by the national price printed on the label.
3. **Cost range of similar models.** The bar under the cost shows where this model sits between the cheapest and most expensive similar models.
4. **Estimated yearly energy use.** Shown in kWh for electric products (or therms for gas). This is the figure to use with your own price.
5. **The fine print.** A line such as "Estimated energy cost based on a national average electricity cost of ___ cents per kWh," plus the usage assumption for that product.
6. **ENERGY STAR logo**, on some labels. On TV labels, the rule allows the logo only for manufacturers that have an agreement covering those TVs with the Department of Energy or the Environmental Protection Agency.

## What Assumptions Are Behind the Label's Cost?

Each product type has its own price and usage assumption.

| Product | Electricity price on new labels | Usage the cost assumes |
|---|---|---|
| Refrigerators and freezers | 14¢/kWh | No usage assumption stated; the label line cites only the price |
| Clothes washers | 14¢/kWh (natural gas $1.21/therm) | "six wash loads a week" |
| Dishwashers | 14¢/kWh | "four wash loads a week" |
| Water heaters | 14¢/kWh (natural gas $1.21/therm) | Not found in the rule text we read |
| Televisions | 16¢/kWh | "5 hours in on mode and 19 hours in standby (sleep) mode per day" |
| Room and portable air conditioners | 13¢/kWh | Not covered here |

Sources: 16 CFR Part 305, including Appendix K1 (14 cents and $1.21 per therm), §305.15 (washers), §305.16 (dishwashers) and §305.25 (TVs); the FTC's October 12, 2022 notice for the 13-cent room air conditioner figure. The 16-cent TV figure took effect May 2, 2024.

For washers, the FTC said in 2022 that the comparison ranges reflect "only the annual energy consumption of the tested model," not the dryer that runs after it.

## Why Does an Old Label Show a Lower Price?

Because the price on a label is frozen when the label goes on. The rule says the cost figure "shall be taken from the appropriate appendix to this part in effect at the time the labels are affixed to the product," and "Products that have been labeled prior to the effective date of a modification under this section need not be relabeled."

So a washer label printed years ago can still say 12 cents. Two Whirlpool washer labels we found read: "Estimated operating cost based on six wash loads a week and a national average electricity cost of 12 cents per kWh and natural gas cost of $1.09 per therm." When you compare two models, check that both labels use the same cents-per-kWh figure. If they do not, compare the kWh instead of the dollars.

## How Much Does the Label Understate Your Cost?

At the U.S. average residential price of 18.16 cents per kWh for August 2026 (EIA, released October 23, 2026; one month's price, not a yearly average):

| Price printed on label | Multiply the label's dollar figure by | Real cost is higher by |
|---|---|---|
| 12¢ | 1.51 | about 51% |
| 13¢ | 1.40 | about 40% |
| 14¢ | 1.30 | about 30% |
| 16¢ | 1.14 | about 14% |

How we calculated it: 18.16 ÷ the label price. If your price is below 18.16 cents, the gap is smaller; in high-price states it is larger. These multipliers only correct the price. They do not change the usage assumption, so a washer you run ten times a week will cost more than either figure.

## How to Calculate Your Own Cost From the Label

**Your yearly cost = label kWh per year × your price per kWh (in dollars)**

1. **Find your price per kWh.** On your bill, add the per-kWh charges for supply and delivery. Leave out the fixed monthly customer charge, which you pay no matter how much you use. Our guide to [reading your electric bill](/posts/how-to-read-your-electric-bill/) shows where each line is.
2. **Multiply.** A washer labeled at 125 kWh a year costs 125 × $0.1816 = **$22.70 a year** at 18.16 cents. Its 12-cent label works out to $15 (125 × $0.12).
3. **Adjust for your use.** If you run the washer 3 loads a week instead of the label's 6, halve it: about $11.35 a year.
4. **Divide by 12 for a monthly figure.** $22.70 ÷ 12 = about $1.89 a month.

Our appliance guides apply the same math with real labels and EIA averages: [refrigerators](/posts/refrigerator-cost-to-run/), [washing machines](/posts/washing-machine-cost-to-run/), [dishwashers](/posts/dishwasher-cost-to-run/) and [TVs](/posts/tv-cost-to-run/).

## Who Should Rely on the Dollar Figure, and Who Should Not

- **Use it** to rank models in the same category, as long as the labels show the same price per kWh.
- **Do not use it** as a budget number. Your price, your usage and the label's age all move the real cost.
- **Solar owners:** an efficient appliance cuts the kWh you need to buy or offset. The value of each kWh saved depends on how your utility credits solar, so use the price you actually avoid paying. Under full retail net metering that is your full per-kWh price; under export credits it can be lower for midday use you would have exported.

## Limitations

- We read the FTC rule as published in the eCFR and Cornell's Legal Information Institute copy on November 22, 2026. The FTC updates label prices from time to time; check the date in the label's fine print.
- We did not find the water heater label's usage wording or a rule statement on whether the washer figure includes electric water heating, so the table leaves those out.
- The 18.16-cent figure is a national average of all residential revenue divided by kWh sold, including fixed charges. Your own per-kWh price may be higher or lower.
