**In California, Florida, Texas, Arizona and Colorado, a homeowners association (HOA) cannot ban rooftop solar on your house, but it can set rules about where the panels go and how they look.** All five have laws that void HOA rules that prohibit solar outright. Virginia is different: an HOA there can prohibit solar if its recorded declaration says so.

The details matter, because they decide how much an HOA can push you around. California caps the extra cost an HOA rule can add to a photovoltaic system at $1,000, Colorado caps it at 10%, and Virginia treats a rule as unreasonable if it adds 5% to the projected cost. If you are weighing solar in one of these states, our pages on [California electricity rates and solar](/rates/california/), [Florida rates and net metering](/rates/florida/), [Texas electricity prices](/rates/texas/), [Arizona utility rate changes](/rates/arizona/), [Colorado rates and solar credits](/rates/colorado/) and [Virginia rate changes](/rates/virginia/) cover what the panels would save. This guide covers whether you can put them up.

This is a plain-language summary of state statutes as published, not legal advice. Laws change, courts interpret them, and your HOA's documents matter. Check the statute linked in each section and talk with a lawyer about a dispute.

## What Can an HOA Control?

In the states covered here, an HOA's power over solar on your own home generally comes down to three things:

1. **Placement and appearance**, within limits on how much the rule can raise cost or cut output.
2. **The approval process**, sometimes with a legal deadline.
3. **Common areas**, such as shared roofs and grounds, where associations usually keep much more control.

What it generally cannot do in five of the six states is adopt or enforce a rule that effectively prohibits solar on your home.

## HOA Solar Rules by State

| State (statute) | Can the HOA ban solar? | Limit on HOA rules | Approval deadline |
|---|---|---|---|
| California (Civil Code 714) | No; restrictions that effectively prohibit or restrict solar are void | Photovoltaic: no more than $1,000 added cost or 10% lost efficiency | Deemed approved if not denied in writing within 45 days |
| Florida (Stat. 163.04) | No | May pick the roof spot within a south-facing range (up to 45° east or west of due south) if it does not impair effective operation | None stated |
| Texas (Property Code 202.010) | No, with listed exceptions | Designated roof area allowed unless another spot raises estimated output more than 10%; roofline, slope and frame-color rules allowed | "Reasonable period" or the period in the HOA's documents |
| Arizona (A.R.S. 33-1816) | No | Placement rules may not impair the device or "adversely affect the cost or efficiency" | None stated |
| Colorado (C.R.S. 38-30-168) | No | Aesthetic rules may not add more than 10% to cost or cut performance more than 10% | Deemed approved if not denied or returned within 60 days |
| Virginia (Code 55.1-1820.1) | Only if the recorded declaration establishes a prohibition | Restriction not reasonable if it adds 5% to projected cost or cuts projected output 10% | None stated |

## California: The $1,000 and 10% Limits

California's Civil Code section 714 says any covenant or restriction "that effectively prohibits or restricts the installation or use of a solar energy system is void and unenforceable." HOAs may still impose "reasonable restrictions," defined as rules that "do not significantly increase the cost of the system or significantly decrease its efficiency or specified performance," or that allow a comparable alternative system.

For a photovoltaic (solar electric) system, "significantly" means more than $1,000 over the system cost "as originally specified and proposed," or a drop in efficiency of more than 10%. Solar water or pool heating has a separate limit: 10% of the system's cost, capped at $1,000.

On process, the HOA must review a solar application "in the same manner as an application for approval of an architectural modification" and may not willfully avoid or delay it. If it does not deny the application in writing within 45 days of receiving it, the application is deemed approved, unless the delay comes from a reasonable request for more information. An HOA that willfully violates the section can owe actual damages and a civil penalty of up to $1,000, and the winning side in a lawsuit gets reasonable attorney's fees.

## Florida: The HOA Can Choose the Roof, Within Limits

Florida Statute 163.04 says a deed restriction, covenant or similar agreement "may not prohibit or have the effect of prohibiting solar collectors" on buildings. An association "may determine the specific location where solar collectors may be installed on the roof," but only "within an orientation to the south or within 45° east or west of due south," and only "if such determination does not impair the effective operation of the solar collectors."

In practice, that means an HOA may be able to steer panels from a street-facing slope to another south-facing slope, but not onto a north-facing roof. The law also says: "This section shall not apply to patio railings in condominiums, cooperatives, or apartments." In a lawsuit under the section, "the prevailing party shall be entitled to costs and reasonable attorney's fees."

## Texas: Prior Approval and a 10% Production Test

Texas Property Code section 202.010 says a property owners' association "may not include or enforce a provision in a dedicatory instrument that prohibits or restricts" a solar energy device, except as the section allows. The exceptions are the most detailed of the six states. An association may prohibit a device that:

- is on property the association owns or on common property;
- "as installed, voids material warranties";
- is located somewhere other than the roof of the home or another permitted structure, or a fenced yard or patio on the owner's property;
- on a roof, "extends higher than or beyond the roofline," "does not conform to the slope of the roof and has a top edge that is not parallel to the roofline," or has a frame, brackets or visible piping "not in a silver, bronze, or black tone";
- in a fenced yard or patio, is taller than the fence line;
- was installed without the association's prior approval.

An association can require roof panels in a designated area, unless another location "increases the estimated annual energy production of the device" by "more than 10 percent above the energy production" of the designated area, as estimated with a National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) modeling tool. Ask your installer to run that comparison if the HOA's preferred spot is shaded or faces the wrong way.

The association must decide "within a reasonable period or within a period specified in the dedicatory instrument," and it may not withhold approval if the device meets the section's rules, unless it determines in writing that the proposed placement would substantially interfere with the use and enjoyment of land by causing unreasonable discomfort or annoyance to persons of ordinary sensibilities. Written approval from all adjoining owners counts as evidence that it does not. During the development period in a development with fewer than 51 planned homes, the developer may prohibit or restrict solar. The section was last amended in 2015 (S.B. 1626), according to the copy we checked.

## Arizona: No Ban, and No Rules That Hurt Cost or Efficiency

Arizona's statute for planned communities, A.R.S. 33-1816, is short. It says "an association shall not prohibit the installation or use of a solar energy device." An association "may adopt reasonable rules regarding the placement of a solar energy device," but those rules may not prevent installation, impair the device's functioning, restrict its use or "adversely affect the cost or efficiency of the device." The court "shall award reasonable attorney fees and costs to any party who substantially prevails" in a lawsuit under the section.

## Colorado: 10% Limits and a 60-Day Clock

Colorado's C.R.S. 38-30-168 says a covenant or similar instrument that "effectively prohibits or restricts the installation or use of a renewable energy generation device" is void and unenforceable. The law covers more than solar; its list also includes certain wind, geothermal and heat pump systems.

Associations may adopt "aesthetic provisions that impose reasonable restrictions on the dimensions, placement, or external appearance" of a device, but only if they do not:

- "Increase the cost of the device by more than ten percent";
- "Decrease the performance or efficiency of the device by more than ten percent"; or
- "Require a period of review and approval that exceeds sixty days after the date of application."

An application not denied or returned for changes within 60 days is deemed approved. A denial "must not be arbitrary or capricious," and "the basis for any denial must be described in reasonable detail." Bona fide safety requirements from building codes or electrical standards still apply. The law gives no right to put a device on a limited or general common element of a common interest community, and the section does not apply to an association that includes time share units.

## Virginia: A Ban Is Possible if the Declaration Says So

Virginia Code 55.1-1820.1 starts from a different place. It says: "No association shall prohibit an owner from installing a solar energy collection device on that owner's property unless the recorded declaration for the association establishes such a prohibition." So the first thing to check in Virginia is your declaration.

Where solar is allowed, the association "may establish reasonable restrictions concerning the size, place, and manner of placement." A restriction is not reasonable if it increases the cost of installation by five percent over the projected cost, or reduces energy production by 10 percent below the projected production, of the owner's initial proposal. To rely on that test, the owner must provide documentation from an independent solar designer who is certified by the North American Board of Certified Energy Practitioners (NABCEP) and licensed in Virginia, and the documentation must be satisfactory to the association. Associations may prohibit or restrict solar on common areas, and the resale certificate for a home must disclose any solar restriction. The current text dates to 2023 amendments. This section is part of Virginia's Property Owners' Association Act; condominium associations are governed by a separate law.

## What This Means in Dollars

The cost limits are easier to judge with a number. Take a hypothetical $25,000 rooftop system (an example, not a typical price):

| State | Most an HOA rule can add, by the statute's test |
|---|---|
| California | $1,000 (photovoltaic cap) |
| Colorado | $2,500 (10% of the device's cost) |
| Virginia | Less than $1,250 (a rule adding 5%, or $1,250, is not reasonable) |

How we calculated it: 10% and 5% of $25,000. Florida, Texas and Arizona do not use a dollar or percentage cost test in the sections quoted here, although Arizona bars rules that "adversely affect the cost." For what a system actually costs, see [how much solar panels cost](/posts/how-much-do-solar-panels-cost/).

## How to Get HOA Approval for Solar

1. **Read your HOA's documents first.** Look for architectural review rules, any solar guidelines and, in Virginia, any prohibition in the recorded declaration.
2. **Ask your installer for a production estimate for each roof location** the HOA might prefer. California, Colorado and Virginia tie the limits to added cost and lost efficiency or output, and Texas to estimated annual production, so numbers help. Our [guide to comparing solar quotes](/posts/how-to-compare-solar-quotes/) covers what an estimate should show.
3. **Submit a complete application in writing** and keep proof of the date it was received. California's 45-day and Colorado's 60-day clocks start from receipt or application.
4. **Fix the roof first if it needs it.** Texas lets an HOA prohibit an installation that voids material warranties; see [whether to replace your roof before solar](/posts/replace-roof-before-solar/).
5. **If you are denied, ask for the reason in writing** and compare it with your state's statute before you appeal or call a lawyer.

## Who This Does Not Cover

- **Other states.** Other states may have their own solar access laws, with different rules, or none. Check your state's statute.
- **Condos and shared roofs.** If the roof is a common element, the association usually has much more control.
- **Local rules.** Historic districts, building permits and utility interconnection are separate from HOA approval.
- **Leases and PPAs.** The HOA rules are the same, but your lease or PPA contract may add its own steps; see [solar lease vs. buying](/posts/solar-lease-vs-buy/).
