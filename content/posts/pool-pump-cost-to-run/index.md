**The average U.S. home with a pool pump used about 2,384 kWh a year on it, according to the Energy Information Administration's (EIA) 2020 survey. At the U.S. average residential price of 18.16 cents per kWh for August 2026, that is about $433 a year, or about $36 a month averaged over 12 months.** Where you live and how you run the pump matter a lot: the same usage costs about $357 a year at Florida's average price and about $709 in California.

A pool pump is often one of the biggest electric loads in a home that has one. ENERGY STAR calls it potentially "your home's second largest energy user." This guide shows the math, how the cost changes by region and state, and the settings that cut it. For the other backyard water load, see our [hot tub cost to run](/posts/hot-tub-cost-to-run/) guide.

## The Formula

**Cost = kWh used × your price per kWh**

Your price per kWh is on your bill. If your bill splits supply and delivery, add the per-kWh parts together and leave out the fixed monthly customer charge. Our [kW vs kWh explainer](/posts/kw-vs-kwh/) covers the units.

## How Much Does It Cost to Run a Pool Pump?

EIA's 2020 Residential Energy Consumption Survey (RECS) reports average electricity use per household that has each end use. For pool pumps, using EIA's August 2026 U.S. average price of 18.16 cents per kWh (released October 23, 2026):

| Period | kWh (EIA 2020 average) | Cost at 18.16¢ |
|---|---|---|
| Per year | 2,384 | $432.93 |
| Per month (yearly total ÷ 12) | about 199 | $36.08 |
| Per day (yearly total ÷ 365) | about 6.5 | $1.19 |

Two cautions. This is a 2020 average across all homes with a pool pump, from small above-ground pools to large in-ground ones, and across climates. And many pools run their pumps only part of the year, so a month in swim season can cost well above the $36 average.

| Northeast | 1,964 | $356.66 | $29.72 |

Homes in the South used the most: their average is about 17% above the national figure.

## How to Lower Your Pool Pump Cost

### Run it slower

Pump energy falls much faster than pump speed. ENERGY STAR puts it this way: "reducing pump speed by one-half allows the pump to use just one-eighth as much energy." It also notes that "Filtration, for example, only requires half the flow rate of running a pool cleaner." That figure is per hour of running. At half speed the pump moves about half as much water, so it needs to run about twice as long for the same filtering, which still works out to roughly one-quarter of the energy (our math). That is why a variable-speed pump, run at low speed for filtering and higher speed only when needed, usually costs less than a single-speed pump running flat out.

ENERGY STAR says certified in-ground pool pumps "use 20% less energy than standard pool pumps and can save approximately $50 a year," and certified above-ground pumps "use 11% less energy than standard pool pumps and can save $20 a year in energy costs." It does not say which electricity price those dollar figures assume.

### Run it fewer hours

ENERGY STAR notes that "Running your pool filter only 6 hours a day versus 24 hours, will reduce your energy use by 75%." If debris is a problem, it suggests "using a timer that can activate the pump for many short periods each day." Run long enough to keep the water clear and the chemistry in range; a green pool costs more to fix than the electricity you saved.

### Check the cleaner and the backwash

According to a PG&E study cited by ENERGY STAR, a robotic cleaner "used only 197 kWh per year," compared with 1,675 kWh for a cleaner driven by the filter pump and 2,989 kWh for one driven by a booster pump. ENERGY STAR also warns that "some filters automatically backwash more frequently than they need to."

### Watch freeze protection settings

Many pumps turn on automatically in cold weather to keep pipes from freezing. Under federal rules, pumps sold with freeze protection must ship with it disabled or with default settings of no more than 40°F, a run time of no more than 1 hour before the temperature is rechecked, and a speed of no more than half the maximum. If an installer raised those settings, the pump may run more on cold nights than you expect.

## What the Federal Pool Pump Standard Means

The Department of Energy's efficiency standards for "dedicated-purpose pool pumps" apply to pumps manufactured starting July 19, 2021. They set a minimum weighted energy factor (gallons pumped per kWh, measured in thousands of gallons) for self-priming and non-self-priming pool filter pumps and pressure cleaner booster pumps. If your pump was made before that date and runs at one speed, compare the weighted energy factor of any replacement, and ask the installer about variable-speed models.

## Pool Pumps, Solar and Time-of-Use Rates

If you have solar panels, running the pump during sunny hours lets your panels power it directly. That saves the full retail price for each kWh, while sending the same kWh to the grid often earns less, especially under net billing rules. If you are on a time-of-use rate without solar, scheduling the pump outside peak hours lowers the cost per kWh.
