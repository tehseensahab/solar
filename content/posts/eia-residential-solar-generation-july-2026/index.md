EIA estimates that homes in the United States generated 7,287 thousand megawatthours (MWh) of electricity from rooftop and other small-scale solar systems in July 2026, up 8.9% from 6,689 thousand MWh in July 2025. California alone accounted for about 37% of that total.

The figures come from Table 1.17.A of the U.S. Energy Information Administration's (EIA) *Electric Power Monthly*, released September 24, 2026. EIA marks them preliminary, and the next release is scheduled for October 23, 2026.

## U.S. Totals at a Glance

| Measure (July) | 2026 | 2025 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Residential small-scale solar | 7,287 | 6,689 | +8.9% |
| All small-scale solar (residential, commercial, industrial) | 10,925 | 9,857 | +10.8% |
| All solar (utility-scale plus small-scale) | 52,022 | 42,913 | +21.2% |

Units are thousand MWh. One thousand MWh is one million kilowatthours (kWh), so 7,287 thousand MWh is about 7.29 billion kWh. The percentage changes for small-scale rows are our calculations from the table's values. The all-solar change (21.2%) is as printed by EIA.

Two ratios put the residential figure in context:

- Residential systems were about **66.7%** of small-scale generation (7,287 ÷ 10,925).
- Small-scale systems were about **21.0%** of all U.S. solar generation (10,925 ÷ 52,022). The remaining roughly 79% came from utility-scale plants.

Utility-scale generation grew far faster than small-scale generation in this comparison. In July 2026 all solar combined rose 21.2%, against 8.9% for residential.

## What EIA Means by "Residential Small-Scale Solar"

EIA's glossary defines small-scale solar as photovoltaic systems smaller than one megawatt, measured in alternating current (AC), the power form that comes out of the inverter, not the DC rating of the panels. EIA then splits its estimate by the type of customer: residential, commercial and industrial.

**Estimated, not metered.** EIA surveys power plants of 1 MW and larger directly (Form EIA-923). For small systems, which are mostly behind customers' meters, it uses utility-reported data (Forms EIA-861M and EIA-861) and its estimation methods. So the numbers are best read as a model-based estimate of generation, including power used on site and power exported to the grid.

## The States With the Most Residential Solar Output

| California | 2,668 | 2,524 | +5.7% | 36.6% |
| Texas | 519 | 489 | +6.1% | 7.1% |
| Florida | 488 | 442 | +10.4% | 6.7% |
| Arizona | 437 | 416 | +5.0% | 6.0% |
| New York | 289 | 257 | +12.5% | 4.0% |
| Massachusetts | 245 | 219 | +11.9% | 3.4% |
| New Jersey | 244 | 231 | +5.6% | 3.3% |
| Nevada | 217 | 208 | +4.3% | 3.0% |
| Colorado | 207 | 170 | +21.8% | 2.8% |
| Illinois | 196 | 162 | +21.0% | 2.7% |

Changes and shares are our calculations from EIA's rounded values, so they can differ slightly from figures computed on unrounded data.

What stands out:

- **Concentration.** California, Texas, Florida and Arizona together were 4,112 thousand MWh, or 56.4% of the national residential estimate.
- **Fast percentage growth outside the biggest markets.** Pennsylvania rose 27.4% (135 vs. 106), Colorado 21.8% and Illinois 21.0%. These are one-month comparisons on smaller bases, and a few thousand MWh moves the percentage noticeably.
- **California grew more slowly than the nation.** Its residential estimate rose 5.7%, below the U.S. 8.9%. EIA's table does not explain why. Possible factors include weather, installation trends and rule changes, and the table cannot distinguish among them.
- **A few states were flat.** Louisiana's residential estimate was 34 vs. 35 thousand MWh. At that size, rounding matters, so we describe it as roughly flat, not as a decline.

California's small-scale share of its own solar output also differs sharply from the national picture. Small-scale systems generated 3,988 thousand MWh of California's 10,385 thousand MWh of solar, or about 38.4%, compared with 21.0% nationally.

## How to Read These Numbers

**One month, one season.** July is near the seasonal peak for solar output in much of the country. A July figure cannot be annualized.

**Capacity versus weather.** A year-over-year increase in generation can come from more installed systems, from a sunnier July, or both. The table reports generation only. It is not evidence that more systems were added in a given state, and it does not show why a state's figure moved.

**Generation is not savings.** The data do not show how much of the power was used on site versus exported, or what customers were paid or credited. That depends on each utility's rules. See [net metering by state](/posts/net-metering-state-guide/) and [export credit rates by utility](/posts/solar-export-credit-rates-by-utility-2026/).

**"NM" means not meaningful.** EIA's table notes use NM for values withheld because of a large standard error or an excessive percentage change. Alabama's residential estimate is one such cell, and New Hampshire and Alaska have gaps in the all-sectors comparison. We did not fill those in.

**Preliminary.** EIA says the values are preliminary and may be revised in later releases.

## What to Watch Next

EIA's next *Electric Power Monthly* is scheduled for October 23, 2026, with August 2026 data. If you compare August to July, remember that the two months differ in length and weather. Compare each to the same month a year earlier.
