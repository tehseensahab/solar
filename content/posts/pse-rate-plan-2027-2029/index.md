Puget Sound Energy (PSE), Washington's largest investor-owned utility, has asked state regulators to approve three annual electric rate increases starting in 2027. For a home using 800 kilowatt-hours (kWh) a month, PSE's own notice to customers shows the monthly bill rising from $159.46 to $210.04 by 2029. The request is pending. **No increase has been approved, and the Washington Utilities and Transportation Commission (UTC) can approve more or less than PSE asked.**

This article covers PSE's electric service, which reaches about 1.2 million customers, according to the Attorney General's office. It does not cover Avista, PacifiCorp, or public utilities such as Seattle City Light, which set their own rates. For what changed on January 1, 2026, see our [Washington rate guide](/posts/washington-electricity-rate-changes-2026/).

## The Request at a Glance

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Filed | February 27, 2026, with the Washington UTC |
| Dockets | UE-260005 (electric) and UG-260006 (natural gas) |
| Electric increase, 2027 | 15.2% overall, about $625 million more in revenue |
| Electric increase, 2028 | 3.7% overall, about $179 million more |
| Electric increase, 2029 | 8.7% overall, about $432 million more |
| Proposed start of 2027 rates | February 2027 (PSE's notice) |
| Status | Pending. No UTC decision |
| Review length | "May take up to 11 months" (PSE's notice) |

Source: PSE's "Notice of public hearings and requested changes to Puget Sound Energy rates." The percentages are PSE's "overall average increase across all usage amounts" for electric customers as a whole, not for one home.

PSE says the plan reflects more than $3.2 billion of planned investment in its gas and electric systems, with more than 70% going to the electric system for safety, reliability, growing demand and storm and wildfire hardening. It also cites a state requirement to supply 80% of electricity from renewable or non-emitting resources by a milestone between 2030 and 2033, and says the plan includes 11 new utility-scale renewable projects.

## What a Typical Home Would Pay

PSE's notice shows the typical residential electric customer using 800 kWh a month:

| | Today (at filing) | Proposed Feb. 2027 | Proposed Feb. 2028* | Proposed Feb. 2029* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Monthly basic charge | $7.49 | $12.50 | $15.00 | $20.00 |
| Energy charge, first 600 kWh | $111.06 | $129.44 | $132.30 | $138.81 |
| Energy charge, over 600 kWh (200 kWh) | $40.90 | $45.83 | $47.05 | $51.23 |
| **Total bill at 800 kWh** | **$159.46** | **$187.77** | **$194.35** | **$210.04** |
| Change from prior step | | +$28.31 | +$6.58 | +$15.69 |

*PSE labels its 2028 and 2029 figures as estimates "for illustration only"; the rates for those years would be filed separately.

The notice's total for today's bill is $159.46; its components add to $159.45, a one-cent rounding difference we left as published.

### The math

- **Per step:** $187.77 − $159.46 = $28.31, or 17.75% (28.31 ÷ 159.46). Then $194.35 − $187.77 = $6.58 (3.50%), and $210.04 − $194.35 = $15.69 (8.07%). These match the percentages in PSE's notice.
- **Total by 2029:** $210.04 − $159.46 = **$50.58 a month**, or **31.7%**. That is $606.96 a year. The Attorney General's office cites the same dollar figures.
- **Averages by rate schedule:** For the main residential schedules (7, 307 and 327), PSE's notice lists the average price per kWh, including the basic charge and across all usage levels, moving from 20.2365¢ to 23.6261¢ in 2027 (+16.75%), then 24.4484¢ (+3.76%) and 26.5603¢ (+8.81%).

### Why you may see 15.2%, 16.75%, 17% and 17.75%

All four figures appear in public documents about the same 2027 step, and they are not contradictory:

- **15.2%** is the average across all electric customers and usage, including businesses.
- **16.75%** is the average price per kWh for the residential schedules at all usage levels.
- **17.75%** is the change in the 800 kWh residential bill that PSE uses as its typical example.
- **17%** is the figure in the Attorney General's July 30 release for the same $28.31 monthly increase. It is a rounded figure; PSE's notice and our division give 17.75%.

Your own change depends on how much electricity you use and which rate schedule you are on. PSE's notice says an individual customer will see a change "greater or less than what is shown."

## How the Increase Adds Up Over Three Years

A rate increase keeps collecting every year after it takes effect. Adding PSE's three electric steps, with each one counted for every year it is in force:

- $625 million × 3 years + $179 million × 2 years + $432 million × 1 year = **about $2.67 billion** over the three rate years.
- Adding the gas steps the same way ($192 million, $48.8 million and $57.8 million) gives about $731 million, for **about $3.40 billion** in total.

That total matches the $3.396 billion over three years that the Attorney General's office says customers would pay extra. Our calculation is a simple sum of PSE's published dollar steps, it assumes each step takes effect for a full year, and it is not a forecast of what customers will pay. It does explain why a plan described as "more than $1.5 billion" in one news report and "$3.4 billion" in another is the same request measured two ways: the first adds each year's *new* revenue once, the second counts what the increases collect over time.

## The Basic Charge: Why Solar Owners Should Look Closely

PSE proposes to raise the monthly basic charge on the electric bill for a typical residential customer from **$7.49** to **$12.50** (2027), **$15.00** (2028) and **$20.00** (2029). That is an increase of $12.51 a month, about 2.7 times today's charge. It accounts for about 25% of the total $50.58 monthly increase (12.51 ÷ 50.58).

A basic charge is a fixed monthly fee. You pay it whether you use 800 kWh or none, and rooftop solar does not reduce it. (For more on this, see [fixed charges and solar](/posts/fixed-charges-and-solar-savings/).)

The per-kWh energy charges rise too, but by a smaller percentage than the bill as a whole. Using PSE's published figures and dividing each block's dollars by its kWh (600 kWh in the first block, 200 in the second):

| | Today | 2027 | 2028* | 2029* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| First 600 kWh, ¢ per kWh | 18.51¢ | 21.57¢ | 22.05¢ | 23.14¢ |
| Over 600 kWh, ¢ per kWh | 20.45¢ | 22.92¢ | 23.53¢ | 25.62¢ |

Our derived rates. They equal PSE's block dollars at 800 kWh divided by the kWh in each block; they are not PSE's tariff rates and exclude any other charges.

### An illustration (not a prediction)

Suppose a home that would use 800 kWh a month has solar that cuts what it buys from PSE to a net 300 kWh in a given month. Assume one-for-one credit for kWh sent to the grid, which is how PSE's Schedule 150 describes net metering (see below), and ignore taxes and other charges. All of the 300 net kWh then fall in the first, lower-priced block:

| | Today | 2027 | 2028* | 2029* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Net bill, 300 kWh | $63.02 | $77.22 | $81.15 | $89.41 |
| Basic charge as share of that bill | 11.9% | 16.2% | 18.5% | 22.4% |
| Bill without solar (800 kWh) | $159.46 | $187.77 | $194.35 | $210.04 |

Working: net bill = basic charge + 300 × the first-block rate. For today, $7.49 + 300 × $0.1851 = $63.02. For 2029, $20.00 + 300 × $0.2314 = $89.41.

The result is that the bill without solar would rise 31.7% by 2029, while the net bill with solar would rise 41.9% ($63.02 to $89.41). The solar home saves *more dollars* each month (about $96 today versus about $121 in 2029 for those 500 offset kWh) because each kWh is worth more. But because a larger fraction of its bill is the fixed charge, its bill grows faster in percentage terms. This is an illustration with invented usage, not a statement about any real customer.

### What PSE's net metering tariff says

PSE's net metering rider, Schedule 150 (9th revision, effective February 29, 2024, the version we read), says that when a customer generates more than it uses in a billing period, the excess kWh are credited and "available to offset electricity consumption during following bill periods." It also says that on March 31 each year, unused kWh credits from the previous year are granted to PSE without compensation. It says new service is available to customers who applied for interconnection until December 31, 2025, "or when a replacement Net Metering schedule is in place, whichever is later."

We could not confirm from a current tariff whether a replacement schedule has since been filed or approved. If you have solar or are quoting a system, read the current Schedule 150 on PSE's website and ask your installer which version applies to your interconnection date. See also our [state net metering guide](/posts/net-metering-state-guide/).

## The Attorney General's Challenge

In Washington, residential and small-business customers are represented in rate cases by the Public Counsel Unit of the state Attorney General's office. In a July 30, 2026 release, Attorney General Nick Brown said his office had filed expert testimony calling PSE's electric (about 29%) and gas (about 20%) increases over three years "excessive and unreasonable."

According to the release, the office believes PSE could save customers **as much as $695 million in 2027 alone** by lowering PSE's return on equity (the profit the utility is allowed to earn on shareholders' investment) to its cost of capital, having shareholders rather than customers pay for items such as insurance, investor relations and executive compensation, and requiring more accurate cost forecasts. The office says it will look for more savings as the case continues.

PSE responded on July 30, according to a September 14 Cascadia Daily report, that claims its requests are about increasing profits "ignore the facts," and a PSE communications director said the company knows "customers are unhappy about higher bills."

The $695 million figure is the Attorney General's estimate, an advocacy position in a contested case. The UTC will weigh PSE's, the Attorney General's and other parties' evidence. The outcome could be any amount.

## What Happens Next

- **Decision:** PSE's notice says the review may take up to 11 months. Counting from February 27, 2026, that would be about late January 2027 (our calculation). We did not find a published decision date, and the UTC could rule earlier.
- **Timing of new rates:** PSE's notice proposes February 2027 for the first step; the Attorney General's release describes the first increase as taking effect in January 2027. We use PSE's notice, which is the filing party's own customer disclosure, but the exact date will be set by the UTC's order.
- **Public hearings:** The UTC scheduled an in-person hearing at 6 p.m. on September 29, 2026 in Lacey and a virtual hearing at 6 p.m. on October 7, 2026. Both dates have passed. The notice lists other ways to comment: the UTC's online comment form, an email to comments@utc.wa.gov, phone 1-888-333-9882, or mail to P.O. Box 47250, Olympia, WA 98504. Cite dockets UE-260005 and UG-260006. We did not verify whether the UTC is still accepting comments in this case.
- **Settlements:** Rate cases can end in negotiated settlements. See [how electric rate cases work](/posts/how-electric-rate-cases-work/).

## What PSE Customers Can Do Now

1. **Nothing changes on your bill yet.** The proposed 2027 rates are not in effect.
2. **Find your own numbers.** Check your rate schedule and typical monthly kWh on your bill (see [how to read your electric bill](/posts/how-to-read-your-electric-bill/)). Then compare against the 800 kWh example.
3. **If you are quoting solar,** ask installers to model the proposed basic charge and energy rates, not only today's. A savings estimate based on today's bill is likely to overstate the net bill reduction if the fixed charge rises. Compare it with the quote checklist in [how to compare solar quotes](/posts/how-to-compare-solar-quotes/).
4. **If bills are a strain,** PSE lists bill assistance programs at PSE.com/GetHelp.

For other utilities with pending requests, see [utility rate requests through Q3 2026](/posts/utility-rate-increase-requests-q3-2026/).

## Limitations

All rates and increases here are proposals by PSE, shown as the company published them. We did not read the full rate case filing, testimony or any UTC order. The 2028 and 2029 figures are labeled by PSE as illustrations. The solar illustration uses invented usage and one-for-one crediting, ignores taxes and other riders, and applies only to PSE customers on a standard residential rate with net metering. The docket title and PSE's notice describe the plan's length differently (four years versus three), and the notice and the Attorney General's release give different timing for the first increase (February versus January 2027). We have flagged both and relied on PSE's notice. This is general information, not financial or legal advice.
