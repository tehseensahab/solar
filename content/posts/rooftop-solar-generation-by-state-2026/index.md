Rooftop solar on U.S. homes produced an estimated **42.9 billion kilowatt-hours (kWh)** of electricity from January through July 2026, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA). That is 11.7% more than the 38.4 billion kWh it estimated for the same seven months of 2025, and about 1.6% of all electricity generated in the country.

A **kilowatt-hour** is the unit of energy on an electric bill. The average U.S. home bought about 874 kWh a month in 2025, according to EIA's annual data (see our [average electric bill by state](/posts/average-electric-bill-by-state-2025/) report). Those figures are for residential customers only; EIA estimates commercial and industrial rooftops separately.

One caution applies to every number below. EIA does not meter rooftop systems one by one. It **estimates** their output from reported capacity. We explain what that means for reading the data in a later section.

## The National Numbers

All figures are EIA estimates for January through July of each year. They are preliminary. 1 billion kWh = 1 terawatt-hour (TWh).

| Measure (U.S., Jan–Jul) | 2025 | 2026 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Utility-scale solar photovoltaic (plants of 1 megawatt or larger) | 175.3 billion kWh | 214.4 billion kWh | +22.3% |
| Small-scale solar photovoltaic, all sectors (under 1 megawatt) | 56.5 billion kWh | 63.6 billion kWh | +12.6% |
| of which residential | 38.4 billion kWh | 42.9 billion kWh | +11.7% |
| of which commercial and industrial | 18.1 billion kWh | 20.7 billion kWh | +14.6% |
| All solar (including a small amount of solar thermal) | 233.6 billion kWh | 279.8 billion kWh | +19.7% |
| All solar as a share of U.S. generation | 8.9% | 10.4% | +1.5 percentage points |
| Residential rooftop solar as a share of U.S. generation | 1.5% | 1.6% | +0.1 percentage points |

**How we calculated the shares:** total U.S. generation is utility-scale generation from all sources plus EIA's estimate of small-scale solar (2026: 2,629.9 + 63.6 = 2,693.5 billion kWh; 2025: 2,578.7 + 56.5 = 2,635.2 billion kWh). Percentage changes and the commercial-and-industrial row (small-scale total minus residential) are our arithmetic from EIA's tables.

Utility-scale solar grew faster than rooftop solar in these data (+22.3% against +12.6%). Small-scale systems are still a meaningful part of the picture: they produced about 23% of all photovoltaic electricity, 63.6 of 278.1 billion kWh.

Over the 12 months ended July 2026, EIA estimates small-scale solar produced 100.3 billion kWh, 12.0% more than the 89.5 billion in the 12 months ended July 2025.

## Which States Produce the Most

California alone accounts for more than a third of estimated residential rooftop output. The five largest states account for 61.7%, and the ten largest for 76.5%.

| Rank | State | Jan–Jul 2026 | Jan–Jul 2025 | Change | Share of U.S. residential |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | California | 15.6 billion kWh | 14.3 billion kWh | +9.4% | 36.3% |
| 2 | Florida | 3.3 billion kWh | 2.9 billion kWh | +12.5% | 7.6% |
| 3 | Texas | 3.1 billion kWh | 2.9 billion kWh | +6.2% | 7.2% |
| 4 | Arizona | 2.9 billion kWh | 2.7 billion kWh | +6.9% | 6.8% |
| 5 | New York | 1.6 billion kWh | 1.4 billion kWh | +14.9% | 3.7% |

The state share is output, not adoption. A large, sunny or populous state produces more in total. EIA's table does not tell us how many homes have solar, how large each system is, or output per home, so it cannot show which state's homeowners are most likely to have panels.

Among the 22 states with at least 300 million kWh of estimated residential output in January through July 2026 (a floor we chose to avoid very small bases), the fastest growth was:

| State | Jan–Jul 2025 | Jan–Jul 2026 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pennsylvania | 569 million kWh | 724 million kWh | +27.2% |
| Illinois | 807 million kWh | 1,017 million kWh | +26.0% |
| Massachusetts | 1,118 million kWh | 1,332 million kWh | +19.1% |
| Connecticut | 702 million kWh | 834 million kWh | +18.8% |
| New Jersey | 1,233 million kWh | 1,454 million kWh | +17.9% |
| Maryland | 767 million kWh | 901 million kWh | +17.5% |

The slowest growth among those 22 states was in South Carolina (+4.3%), New Mexico (+5.4%), Texas (+6.2%), Utah (+6.4%) and Arizona (+6.9%). No state's estimate fell on EIA's published, rounded figures.

EIA's tables show *how much* each estimate changed, not *why*. The pattern of faster growth in the Northeast and Illinois is consistent with newer installations in those markets, but this data cannot confirm that, and we have not attributed it to any state policy. ## How to Read These Numbers

- **They are estimates, not meter readings.** EIA's technical notes say monthly small-scale solar generation "is an estimation of the generation produced from PV solar resources and not the results of a data collection effort for generation directly," with a limited exception for some third-party-owned systems.
- **The estimate starts from capacity.** Utilities report solar capacity to EIA, which models it for non-sampled utilities and then converts capacity to generation using typical output simulations for each location, based on a model from the National Renewable Energy Laboratory. Our reading of that method is that growth in the estimate mostly follows growth in installed capacity, and year-to-year weather differences are not measured. This is our interpretation of EIA's description, not an EIA statement.
- **It is generation, not savings or exports.** The figures do not say how much of this power homeowners used themselves and how much went to the grid.
- **Growth in output is not growth in new installations.** A fleet that grows 11.7% in a year could include systems installed over many years. This article does not report how many systems were installed in 2026 and the EIA table cannot answer that.
- **"Residential" is EIA's customer class.** Home systems are in it. Commercial and industrial rooftops are in other classes.
- **Data are preliminary.** EIA labels 2025 and 2026 values as preliminary, and its next Electric Power Monthly is scheduled for October 23, 2026.

## What It Means for Homeowners

Aggregate numbers say little about whether solar pays off at a particular address. They do show the market is large and growing from a big base, and that a few states dominate it. For a home, the figures that matter are your utility's rate and export credit, your usage, and the quoted system price and output. ## Limitations

This article covers January through July 2026 only and compares it with the same months of 2025. It uses preliminary EIA estimates that may be revised. We did not find a primary source for the number of homes with solar or for 2026 installation counts, so we do not report them. We did not examine why individual states' estimates changed.
