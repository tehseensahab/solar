The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) estimates that small-scale solar photovoltaic (PV) systems generated 63.6 billion kilowatt-hours (kWh) of electricity from January through July 2026. That is 12.6% more than the 56.5 billion kWh it estimates for the same seven months of 2025. The figures come from Table 1.17.B of EIA's *Electric Power Monthly*, released September 24, 2026.

"Small-scale" is EIA's term for solar systems with less than 1 megawatt (MW) of generating capacity, usually located where the electricity is used. Rooftop systems account for the majority. In this table, 67.4% of the small-scale total (42.9 of 63.6 billion kWh) is in the residential sector; the rest is mainly commercial and industrial buildings.

## The National Picture

| Measure | 2025 | 2026 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Small-scale solar generation, January–July (billion kWh) | 56.5 | 63.6 | +12.6% |
| Small-scale solar capacity at end of July (MW) | 56,307 | 62,840 | +11.6% |
| Capacity added, January–July (MW) | 3,079 | 3,335 | +8.3% |

Capacity is EIA's "net summer" capacity, an estimate for small-scale systems. The capacity figures are from Table 6.1.A of the same publication. The percentages are our calculations from EIA's figures.

Two readings of these numbers deserve care.

**Rooftop solar kept growing in 2026, the first year without the federal homeowner credit.** The IRS says the Residential Clean Energy Credit is not available for property placed in service after December 31, 2025. EIA's preliminary figures still show 3,335 MW added in January–July 2026, slightly more than the 3,079 MW in the same months of 2025. That does not show the credit's end had no effect. The EIA estimate covers all small-scale systems, including commercial ones, it is preliminary, and EIA's table gives no reasons. For homeowners, see our guide to [solar payback without the federal tax credit](/posts/solar-payback-period-without-federal-tax-credit/).

**The generation growth tracks capacity, not weather.** Generation rose 12.6% and capacity rose 11.6%, which are close, but that is by construction. EIA's technical notes explain that small-scale generation is "an estimation" rather than a data collection, built by multiplying reported capacity by an output rate based on typical-year (TMY3) sunshine for each utility's area. A cloudier or sunnier 2026 would not show up in these numbers. Do not use this table to judge how a particular year's weather affected solar output.

Concentration is high. California alone is 36.2% of the U.S. total, the top five states are 58.9%, and the top ten are 75.4%. California also supplied the largest part of the growth: its 2,277 million kWh increase is 32% of the 7,116 million kWh national increase. Excluding California, the rest of the country grew 13.5%, faster than California's 11.0%.

Illinois leads at +33.1%, and its residential-sector estimate rose 26.0% (1,017 vs. 807 million kWh). EIA's table does not say why, and we have not established a cause, so we draw no conclusion about state policy. For Illinois utility rates, see our [Illinois electricity rate changes](/posts/illinois-electricity-rate-changes-2026/) article.

Growth was slowest in a few small markets: Tennessee was flat (52 vs. 52 million kWh), and South Dakota fell from 6 to 4 million kWh, though EIA's own rounding makes changes of a few million kWh meaningless at that size.

## What These Numbers Mean for a Homeowner

Very little about your own system, and that is the point. State totals reflect how many systems exist and how sunny the state is, not what a new system would earn you. What decides a new system's value is your utility's rate and how it credits exported power. Those differ by utility, often more than by state. Our [state net metering guide](/posts/net-metering-state-guide/) and [export credit comparison](/posts/solar-export-credit-rates-by-utility-2026/) cover that, and [how to compare solar quotes](/posts/how-to-compare-solar-quotes/) shows how to test an installer's savings claims against your own bill. California readers can see rates in our [California electricity rate changes](/posts/california-electricity-rate-changes-2026/) article.

## Limitations

- **Estimates.** EIA states that small-scale values are preliminary and estimated from Forms EIA-861M and EIA-861 and modeling. Only generation by some third-party-owned systems is directly reported, and EIA says that data is not comprehensive.
- **Year-to-date only.** Table 1.17.B reports January–July totals, not single months, so we cannot show seasonal patterns.
- **Capacity for the nation only.** We use national capacity (Table 6.1.A). We did not find a state-level capacity series in the same release.
- **Not all rooftop.** The category includes ground-mounted and commercial systems under 1 MW.
- **Next update.** EIA's next *Electric Power Monthly* release is scheduled for October 23, 2026, and earlier months can be revised.
