**FPL's standard residential rate (RS-1) costs about 12.3 cents per kWh for the first 1,000 kWh you use each month and about 14.3 cents for every kWh above that, plus a $10.52 base charge, before taxes and fees.** Those figures add up every per-kWh line on FPL's rate sheet effective October 2026, the latest we found posted as of November 19, 2026. On the optional time-of-use rider, on-peak power costs about 27 cents per kWh and off-peak about 6 cents for the first 1,000 kWh, and peak hours change with the season.

This page is a line-by-line guide to the FPL rate itself. For how Florida's other utilities and the statewide average compare, see our [Florida electric rate tracker](/rates/florida/). If the kW and kWh units on your bill are confusing, start with [kW vs kWh](/posts/kw-vs-kwh/).

## What Is FPL's Residential Electricity Rate in 2026, in Cents per kWh?

FPL bills most homes on rate RS-1. The rate sheet lists a base charge and several per-kWh charges. Two of them, the energy charge and the fuel charge, are 1 cent higher for every kWh above 1,000 in a month. Here is every line, from FPL's rate sheet effective October 2026.

| RS-1 charge | First 1,000 kWh | Above 1,000 kWh |
|---|---|---|
| Base charge (per month) | $10.52 | $10.52 |
| Energy (non-fuel) | 7.865¢ | 8.865¢ |
| Fuel | 2.893¢ | 3.893¢ |
| Conservation | 0.148¢ | 0.148¢ |
| Capacity | 0.052¢ | 0.052¢ |
| Environmental | 0.345¢ | 0.345¢ |
| Storm protection | 0.995¢ | 0.995¢ |
| Transition credit | -0.040¢ | -0.040¢ |
| **All-in per kWh (our sum)** | **12.258¢** | **14.258¢** |

The sum for the first 1,000 kWh: 7.865 + 2.893 + 0.148 + 0.052 + 0.345 + 0.995 - 0.040 = 12.258 cents. Above 1,000 kWh: 8.865 + 3.893 + 0.148 + 0.052 + 0.345 + 0.995 - 0.040 = 14.258 cents.

These are FPL's own charges only. Your bill also carries taxes and local fees, which vary by where you live and are not on the rate sheet.

### What does 1,000 or 1,500 kWh cost on FPL?

Our estimates from the October 2026 rate sheet, before taxes and fees:

| Monthly use | Calculation | FPL charges before taxes and fees |
|---|---|---|
| 1,000 kWh | $10.52 + (1,000 × 12.258¢) | **$133.10** |
| 1,500 kWh | $133.10 + (500 × 14.258¢) | **$204.39** |

At 1,500 kWh, your average cost is about 13.6 cents per kWh ($204.39 ÷ 1,500), because the last 500 kWh are billed at the higher tier. FPL's own figure for its typical 1,000 kWh residential bill in 2026, which includes taxes and fees, is $136.64, according to the company's November 20, 2025 announcement of the rate agreement.

### Why was my September 2026 bill lower?

FPL's September 2026 rate sheets (the Northwest Florida sheet is linked below) carried a one-month "Storm Restoration Recovery Refund" of -0.797 cents per kWh. The October sheet has no refund line. We covered that change in the [October Florida rate update](/rates/florida/).

## What Are FPL's Time-of-Use Hours?

FPL's optional time-of-use rider for homes is called RTR-1. Its tariff sheet (Sheet No. 8.203, effective January 1, 2026) defines on-peak hours this way:

| Season | On-peak hours (Monday to Friday) | Holidays excluded from on-peak |
|---|---|---|
| April 1 to October 31 | 12 noon to 9 p.m. ET | Memorial Day, Independence Day, Labor Day |
| November 1 to March 31 | 6 a.m. to 10 a.m. ET and 6 p.m. to 10 p.m. ET | Thanksgiving Day, Christmas Day, New Year's Day |

"All other hours" are off-peak, which includes all weekend hours. So today, in the winter season, the cheap window covers midday on weekdays (10 a.m. to 6 p.m.) and overnight (10 p.m. to 6 a.m.).

The tariff states the hours in Eastern time. If you live in the Central-time part of Northwest Florida, confirm your local peak hours with FPL before you plan around them. If the ET hours apply as written, noon to 9 p.m. ET is 11 a.m. to 8 p.m. Central.

### How much do on-peak and off-peak kWh cost on FPL?

RTR-1 does not replace RS-1. You pay all the RS-1 charges, and then an adder or credit depending on when you used the power. From FPL's October 2026 rate sheet:

| RTR-1 adder | On-peak | Off-peak |
|---|---|---|
| Base energy | +14.410¢ | -6.157¢ |
| Fuel | +0.226¢ | -0.098¢ |
| **Net change vs RS-1** | **+14.636¢** | **-6.255¢** |
| All-in, first 1,000 kWh (our sum) | 26.894¢ | 6.003¢ |
| All-in, above 1,000 kWh (our sum) | 28.894¢ | 8.003¢ |

The base charge is the same $10.52. FPL's [time-of-use page](https://www.fpl.com/rates/time-of-use.html) rounds this to "about 26 cents" on-peak and "around 9 cents" off-peak and notes those figures may vary by location. Our numbers come straight from the rate sheet and exclude taxes and fees.

## Does FPL Time-of-Use Save Money? The 30% Rule

Because the on-peak adder (+14.636 cents) is larger than the off-peak credit (-6.255 cents), time-of-use only pays off if most of your power is used off-peak. The break-even point is where the two cancel out:

on-peak share = 6.255 ÷ (14.636 + 6.255) = **about 30%**

If less than about 30% of your monthly kWh fall in on-peak hours, RTR-1 lowers your bill. Above 30%, it raises it. For a home using 1,500 kWh a month (our math, before taxes and fees):

| Share of kWh used on-peak | Change vs standard RS-1 |
|---|---|
| 20% | about $31 lower |
| 27% | about $9 lower |
| 40% | about $32 higher |

For context, on-peak hours make up about 27% of all the hours in a summer week (45 of 168) and about 24% in a winter week (40 of 168). A home that used power evenly around the clock would land just under the break-even line. Summer air conditioning tends to run hardest in the afternoon and early evening, which is on-peak, so in our estimate many Florida homes will land above 30% unless they shift some use.

### Who FPL time-of-use suits, and who it does not

FPL's own guidance says that "to realize savings, customers must use less energy during the 'peak' hours." The rate tends to suit:

- Homes that are empty on weekday afternoons and can raise the thermostat (FPL suggests 78 degrees or higher) during peak hours.
- Homes that run pool pumps, dishwashers, laundry and EV charging on timers at night or on weekends.
- Larger users. FPL's examples point to households using more than 1,000 kWh a month. Our reasoning: a bigger home has more off-peak use to earn the credit.

It usually does not suit people who work from home or are home with children on weekday afternoons in summer, or anyone who cannot move air-conditioning load.

Before you switch, know the terms. The tariff's term of service is "not less than one year." FPL says enrollment takes about three months, requires a new meter, has no extra cost, and cannot be combined with FPL On Call, Budget Billing or FPL EVolution.

## What Is the FPL Minimum Bill?

FPL's rate sheet says: "Residential customers whose monthly base electric service costs fall below $30 are subject to a minimum $30 base bill." The RS-1 and RTR-1 tariff sheets both list "Minimum: $30.00."

The rate sheet does not list every charge that counts toward that $30. If it is the base charge plus the non-fuel energy charge, a home reaches $30 at about 248 kWh a month ((30 - 10.52) ÷ 7.865¢). That is our reading, not FPL's. Most full-time homes use far more, so the minimum mainly matters for part-time residences and for homes where solar covers nearly all their usage.

## Does Northwest Florida Pay the Same FPL Rate?

Mostly, but not exactly. FPL's September 2026 rate sheet for Northwest Florida (the former Gulf Power area) shows the same base charge and the same energy, fuel, conservation, capacity, environmental and storm protection charges as the rest of FPL. The difference is a "Transition Rider" of +0.421 cents per kWh instead of the -0.040 cent transition credit elsewhere. Using that sheet, without its one-month September refund, the Northwest Florida all-in rate is about 12.719 cents for the first 1,000 kWh (our math). FPL said in November 2025 that its typical Northwest Florida 1,000 kWh bill would be $141.36 in 2026. We did not find an October or November 2026 Northwest Florida sheet, so check the current one on FPL's site.

## What Changes in January 2027?

On October 30, 2026, FPL said it had filed proposals with the Florida Public Service Commission that would make "the typical 1,000-kWh residential customer bill... $141.87 beginning in January 2027." FPL had projected $143.05 for 2027 in filings last year.

| Typical FPL 1,000 kWh bill (taxes and fees included) | Amount | Status |
|---|---|---|
| 2026 | $136.64 | In effect (agreement approved Nov. 20, 2025) |
| January 2027, earlier projection | $143.05 | FPL's 2025 projection |
| January 2027, current proposal | $141.87 | Proposed; needs PSC approval |

The $141.87 is $5.23 a month (about 3.8%) more than the 2026 typical bill. FPL says the proposals include lower fuel charges because of lower projected natural gas costs, previously approved base rate adjustments, and a new temporary surcharge of $3.48 a month for a typical 1,000 kWh customer from January through December 2027. The surcharge would recover Hurricane Isaias restoration costs and partly refill the storm reserve. FPL says all of this is "subject to PSC review and approval." We found no PSC decision as of November 19, 2026.

When the January 2027 rate sheet is posted, every number in the tables above will change. We will update this page.

## What FPL's Rates Mean If You Have or Want Solar

Three parts of the rate design matter for solar:

1. **The 1,000 kWh tier.** Above 1,000 kWh, each kWh costs 14.258 cents instead of 12.258 cents. Solar that offsets your own use within the billing month cuts the top-tier kWh first, so a high-use home saves more per kWh on its top slice.
2. **The $30 minimum.** Depending on how exports are credited, a system sized to wipe out nearly all your usage can still leave you with the minimum bill.
3. **Time-of-use timing.** FPL's winter peak windows (6 to 10 a.m. and 6 to 10 p.m. ET) fall mostly outside the hours when panels produce, and the summer peak runs until 9 p.m. ET, into the evening, when panel output is low. Solar alone does not cover the expensive evening hours. A battery that shifts midday solar into the evening is the usual way to work around that; see [how time-of-use rates change the value of solar](/posts/time-of-use-rates-and-solar/) and [batteries for time-of-use rates](/posts/best-home-batteries-time-of-use-rates/).

We did not review FPL's net metering rules for this article. Ask FPL how exports are credited before you size a system or join RTR-1 with solar. **Update, Nov. 20, 2026:** we have since covered them in [FPL net metering: how solar credits work](/posts/fpl-net-metering/). Under [FPL's tariff](https://www.fpl.com/rates/pdf/electric-tariff-section10.pdf), excess kWh offset use in later months for up to 12 months, and credits left at year-end are paid at a COG-1 avoided-cost rate.
