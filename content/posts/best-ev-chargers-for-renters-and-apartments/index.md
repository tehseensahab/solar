## Best for: pick by situation

### You have only a standard outlet: use Level 1
DOE says charging overnight with Level 1 meets the daily needs of many owners if there is an outlet on a dedicated branch circuit near the parking spot. Tesla lists 4 to 6 miles per hour on a 120 V outlet depending on model. Math: 10 hours × 5 mi/h = about 50 miles of range overnight, which covers many commutes but not heavy driving.

### You have a dryer or RV-style 240 V outlet nearby: portable Level 2
The Tesla Mobile Connector is 20 feet long and includes NEMA 5-15 and NEMA 14-50 adapters. On a 14-50 outlet at up to 32 A, Tesla lists 30 miles per hour for the Model 3, 27 for the Model S and Y, and 23 for the Model X. Optional adapters for other outlets (for example NEMA 6-20 at 15 mi/h for Model 3) are sold separately. Our math: 8 hours × 27 mi/h = about 216 miles of range overnight on a 14-50 for a Model Y, if the circuit and outlet are in good condition.

### You want a wall unit you can take with you: plug-in Level 2
ChargePoint's Home Flex NEMA 6-50 and 14-50 versions deliver up to 40 A and are listed at about 30 miles per hour. Per ChargePoint, the page states UL listing, ENERGY STAR certification and a 3-year warranty. The Grizzl-E Classic 40A ($299.99, plug-in) is a lower-cost choice, but the page we read did not name the certifying body, warranty or cable length. The Emporia Pro ($599) lists a NEMA plug or hardwire option, 25 ft cable and a 3-year warranty; certifications are not confirmed for the Pro on its page.

## What to ask your landlord or HOA

1. May I use the existing outlet at my space? Is it on a dedicated circuit?
2. Who pays for the electricity if the outlet is on a shared meter?
3. May a licensed electrician add a 240 V outlet, and who pays?
4. Is there a building charging program or a planned shared charger?

Get answers in writing. Rules differ by state, city and building, and we have not verified tenant-charging rules state by state, so check yours.

## Safety notes

Use only the outlets and adapters the manufacturer specifies. Avoid extension cords unless the manufacturer says a particular cord is acceptable. EV charging is a continuous load under the National Electrical Code, per [DOE](https://afdc.energy.gov/fuels/electricity-charging-home), so the circuit and outlet must be sound. Have a licensed electrician inspect any outlet you plan to use at 240 V.

## Who should skip a home charger

- If you can charge at work or at a public charger regularly, a home unit may be unnecessary.
- If the only available outlet is shared or not on a dedicated circuit, ask first.
- If you move often, favor portable options.

## Incentives

The IRS lists the 30C charger credit (30% up to $1,000 per item for individuals) for property placed in service through June 30, 2026, for property at your main home in eligible census tracts. It does not cover equipment placed in service after that date. State and utility rebates may apply; see [Rates by State](/rates-by-state/) and check your utility. Confirm eligibility with a tax professional.

## Buying checklist

1. Outlet type and amperage at your parking space (120 V or 240 V).
2. Your car's connector (J1772 or NACS) and AC charge rate.
3. Landlord or HOA approval in writing.
4. Cable length to reach your parking space safely.
5. Stated UL listing for the exact model.
6. Electricity rate and metering; see [how to read your electric bill](/posts/how-to-read-your-electric-bill/).
7. Compare with [Level 2 chargers for home](/posts/best-level-2-ev-chargers-for-home/) if you may buy a house later.
