**A home with average U.S. electricity use needs roughly 15 to 23 solar panels to cover its yearly use, assuming 430-watt panels.** The exact number comes from three figures: how many kWh you use in a year, how much each kilowatt of panels produces where you live, and the wattage of the panels you buy. The method below takes about ten minutes with a year of electric bills.

This guide works out a national example. Local sunshine, your utility's credit for exported solar and any size limits will move the answer, so treat it as a way to check an installer's proposal, not a replacement for a site design.

## Step 1: Find Your Yearly Electricity Use

Add up the kWh on your last 12 bills, or find the 12-month total that many utilities print on the bill or show online. Use a full year, because heating and cooling swing usage by season.

For comparison, EIA says the average U.S. residential customer bought 10,791 kWh in 2022, "an average of about 899 kWh per month." The range between states is wide: from 14,774 kWh a year in Louisiana to 6,178 kWh in Hawaii.

If you plan to add an electric vehicle, heat pump or heat pump water heater, add its expected yearly kWh now. Our guide on [why bills climb](/posts/why-is-my-electric-bill-so-high/) covers the biggest loads to check.

## Step 2: Estimate How Much Each Kilowatt Produces

A solar panel's watt rating is its output under standard test conditions. Over a year, real output depends on sunshine, roof angle, shading and losses. Planners use a capacity factor: the share of the year's hours a system would need to run at full rated power to match its actual production.

The National Renewable Energy Laboratory's 2023 Annual Technology Baseline (ATB) models a "7.9-kWDC, fixed tilt, roof-mounted system." Its first-year capacity factors "range from 12.7% for Class 10" (the least sunny locations) "to 19.6% for Class 1." The simple average of the ten classes is 15.7%. These are per kW of panels (DC).

Turned into kWh, with 8,760 hours in a year:

| Location type (NREL class) | Capacity factor | kWh per kW of panels per year |
|---|---|---|
| Sunniest (Class 1) | 19.6% | about 1,717 |
| Mid-range (average of NREL's classes) | 15.7% | about 1,375 |
| Least sunny (Class 10) | 12.7% | about 1,113 |

For your own address, NREL's free [PVWatts calculator](https://pvwatts.nrel.gov/) estimates output for your roof's direction and tilt. That is more accurate than a national class.

## Step 3: Turn kWh Into Panels

The math is two divisions:

1. **System size (kW) = yearly kWh ÷ kWh per kW per year**
2. **Number of panels = system size in watts ÷ panel wattage**, rounded up

For the example panel we used a 430-watt Qcells Q.TRON BLK M-G2+ module, which a distributor lists at 1,722 by 1,134 mm. Wattage varies by model, so check the panel wattage on your quote.

For an average home using 10,791 kWh a year:

| Location | Size needed (kW DC) | 430-watt panels | Actual size | Estimated yearly output |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sunniest (1,717 kWh/kW) | 6.3 kW | 15 | 6.45 kW | about 11,070 kWh |
| Mid-range (1,375 kWh/kW) | 7.8 kW | 19 | 8.17 kW | about 11,240 kWh |
| Least sunny (1,113 kWh/kW) | 9.7 kW | 23 | 9.89 kW | about 11,000 kWh |

Rounding up to whole panels means each system slightly overshoots 10,791 kWh. All figures are our arithmetic from the EIA and NREL numbers above, before shading or panel aging.

The mid-range answer is close to what installers actually build. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory reports that "the median U.S. residential system size was 7.7 kW in 2025," which is about 18 panels of 430 watts.

## Check the Roof

At about 21 square feet per panel (1,722 × 1,134 mm), 19 panels cover roughly 400 square feet of panel surface. Real layouts need more: gaps between rows, fire-code setbacks from ridges and edges, and space around vents and skylights. In the U.S., south-facing roofs usually produce the most per panel and east- or west-facing roofs less, so you need more panels for the same kWh. If your roof is nearing the end of its life, read our guide on [whether to replace your roof before solar](/posts/replace-roof-before-solar/).

## When a Smaller System Makes More Sense

A 100% offset is a common target, not a rule. Size down if:

- **Your utility pays less for exports.** Some utilities credit power you send to the grid below the retail rate. Panels that mostly export earn less than panels whose output you use. See our [export credit comparison](/posts/solar-export-credit-rates-by-utility-2026/).
- **Your state caps system size.** Michigan, for example, lets solar customers install generation "up to 110% of customer's electricity consumption for the previous 12 months," according to the Michigan Public Service Commission.
- **Fixed charges are a big part of your bill.** Solar cannot reduce them, so they cap the savings no matter how many panels you add.
- **You want to add a battery.** A battery can shift midday output to the evening; size it separately with our [home battery sizing guide](/posts/how-to-size-a-home-battery/).

Then compare quotes on the same basis: system size in kW, panel wattage and count, and the estimated first-year kWh. Our guide to [comparing solar quotes](/posts/how-to-compare-solar-quotes/) shows what to ask.

## Limitations

The NREL capacity factors are modeled national classes for a fixed-tilt roof system in its first year; your roof's direction, tilt, shading and panel aging will change real output. EIA's usage figure is from 2022 and counts electricity bought from the utility, so it can understate total use in homes that already have solar. We used one example panel; panels with a different wattage change the count but not the system size in kW.
